🐐 Milk Goats, Home Dairy & Bees

How to keep dairy goats healthy with no vet or feed store, get clean milk and keep it without a fridge, turn it into butter, ghee, yogurt, kefir and cheese, and keep honey bees for honey and wax. The books are in Animals\Goats-Sheep, Animals\Dairy-Cheese, Animals\Bees and Animals\Veterinary, with the old farm butter and cheese bulletins in Homestead-Skills\Food-Crafts-Tools and milk cooling in Food-Storage\Cooling-Without-Power.

⚠️ Rules that prevent the worst outcomes

  • Raw milk can carry brucellosis, tuberculosis and Listeria (which causes miscarriage). Pasteurize milk for children, pregnant women, the old and the sick: 145 °F for 30 minutes, or 161 °F for 15 seconds, then cool it fast (UMN, p. 1).
  • Never give honey to a baby under 1 year — infant botulism (FDA Bad Bug Book, p. 108).
  • Never feed hedge or garden trimmings. Yew kills before any sign shows; rhododendron, azalea and oleander poison goats (WSU, p. 2).
  • Oregon is short of selenium. Keep a covered selenium goat mineral out all year, and never let sheep eat goat mineral — its copper poisons them.
  • Shut goats in every night (cougars) and put an electric fence around the hives (black bears).
  • Anyone who has had a whole-body reaction to a sting must not work bees without epinephrine at hand.

🐐 Choosing and feeding milk goats

What to expect: a doe giving about 1,000 lb (about 500 quarts) in a 10-month lactation is a fair milker; 1,600 lb or more is excellent (USDA FB 920, p. 5). That is about 1½ quarts a day (our arithmetic). Two does bred a few months apart keep milk coming all year (rule of thumb).

Breeds (FB 920, p. 10): Saanen, Toggenburg, Nubian and Alpine (Saanen, Toggenburg, Nubian, Alpine). Nubians give the richest milk, best for butter and cheese. LaMancha and the small Nigerian Dwarf are newer (rule of thumb).

Feeding (FB 920, pp. 25–26):

  • Goats browse: they prefer leaves, twigs and weeds to grass.
  • Grain: about 1 lb for every 3–4 lb of milk.
  • One doe for a year: about 500 lb of hay and 450 lb of grain with 6 months of pasture; about twice the hay with none. One acre of good pasture carries 2–3 goats for a 5–6-month season.
  • House the buck away from milking does — his smell gets into the milk.

Here (rule of thumb): pasture browns from June to the October rains and winter is mud, so plan hay for both. Browse: blackberry, willow, maple and oak leaves. Goats eat poison oak; its oil rubs off their coats onto your hands. Change feed slowly, over 7–10 days.

Plants that kill goats — mostly hungry goats in unfamiliar brush (the "where" column is local knowledge, rule of thumb):

Plant Where What happens
Western azalea, rhododendron, laurel Along valley creeks and serpentine meadows; gardens Drooling, vomiting (rare in goats — a red flag), bloat, tremors, collapse; activated charcoal may help (WSU, p. 15)
Pacific yew, garden yew Shady creek forest; hedges Often fatal before any sign (AIB 415, p. 113)
Oleander SW Oregon yards All parts very poisonous (OSU, p. 12)
Chokecherry, cherry Wilted or frosted leaves Cyanide (AIB 415, p. 26)

🏠 Fences, shelter and minerals

  • Fences: no barbed wire. Field fence traps horned heads. Electric works once goats are trained: two hot wires held mature does but not kids (OSU EC1646, pp. 1–2).
  • Shelter: a dry three-sided shed with fresh air but no drafts, and something to climb (shelter, climbing structure). Keep goats dry and out of cold wind (FB 920, p. 35).
  • Cougars: shut goats in a shed or barn every night (ODFW, p. 2).

Selenium (OSU EM 9094, pp. 1–6):

  • Most Oregon livestock are low in selenium (map). White muscle disease was first described here, in 1958. It strikes kids 3–8 weeks old: stiffness, trembling, weak hind legs, can't stand or suckle, heart failure. Low selenium also causes abortion, stillbirth and more mastitis.
  • Free-choice selenium mineral salt is simplest. Keep the feeder covered — rain leaches selenium out.
  • Injections (such as Bo-Se) need a vet, last under a month, and an overdose kills.
  • Stock a year or more of selenium goat mineral now (rule of thumb).

Copper: copper oxide wire particles help control barber pole worm in goats, but copper poisons sheep. Never feed goat mineral to sheep, and don't use low-copper sheep mineral for goats (UF VM271, p. 2).

🍼 Breeding, kidding and kids

What to know
Heat Fall and early winter; every 18–21 days; lasts 24–36 hours. Bleating, tail wagging, swollen vulva, less milk (Peace Corps Vol 4, p. 419)
First breeding Doelings at 7–8 months, not earlier (same page)
Gestation 146–152 days; twins usual, triplets common (FB 920, p. 24). Fall breeding = kids February–April (rule of thumb)

The first hour (KSU MF3689, pp. 11, 13):

  1. "Clip, dip, strip, sip": cut the navel cord under 2 inches and dip it in iodine; strip the wax plugs from the doe's teats; make sure the kid nurses.
  2. Colostrum within 30–60 minutes, at least 10% of body weight: a 10-lb kid needs about 7 oz in the first hour and 16 oz over 24 hours, in 2–4-oz feeds (tube-feeding photos).
  3. Kid under 99 °F? Warm it first — a chilled kid can't absorb colostrum.
  4. Never pull on the afterbirth. For a stuck kid see the Langston guide (normal and wrong positions; KSU drawings).

Bottle kids (FB 920, pp. 28, 31): warm (90–98 °F) milk, as much as they drink readily — at least 1½–2 pints a day, often 2–3 — in 3 feeds, until 6 weeks; after 10 weeks hay and grain replace most of it; wean at 3–4 months. Use the doe's milk for people only from the 4th–5th day after kidding.

Disbudding (FB 920, p. 34):

  1. In the first days of life, always before 3 weeks. On a naturally hornless kid the skin slides over the spot.
  2. Heat a disbudding iron (or a ¾-inch steel pipe on a handle) cherry-red (where to burn). Hold it on each bud 5–10 seconds, rotating, until a copper-colored ring shows. Keep it as short as you can: newer studies found brain injury in kids even at 5 seconds, so have someone experienced show you first.
  3. Give tetanus antitoxin. Meloxicam (prescription) eases the pain (KSU, p. 15).

🩺 Goat health with no vet

Problem Signs What to do
CAE Swollen knees, wasting; a fatal brain form in kids. Spread by colostrum and milk No cure. Buy test-negative goats. Take kids from infected does at birth; feed heat-treated colostrum and pasteurized milk (Idaho, p. 1) — heat colostrum 1 hour at 135 °F (rule of thumb)
CL Lumps behind the ear, under the jaw, on neck, shoulder, flank or udder (abscess, in a sheep). The pus infects people too Isolate; lance and drain away from the herd; disinfect tools (Idaho, p. 1). Gloves; burn the bedding (rule of thumb)
Mastitis Hot, hard, lopsided udder; watery, clotted or bloody milk Milk that half out 4+ times a day; warm wet cloths; antibiotics if you have them, then don't drink her milk for several days — at least the milk withdrawal time on the drug's label (p. 176). Never push straw or sticks up a teat (No Animal Doctor, p. 175)
Milk fever Around kidding: weak, cold to the touch, can't stand Calcium gluconate under the skin in several places; into a vein only slowly, listening to the heart. No alfalfa-heavy feed late in pregnancy (p. 289)
Pregnancy toxemia Doe with twins or triplets, last weeks: stops eating, stands alone, presses head into walls Propylene glycol by mouth, about 60 mL (2 oz) twice a day (Merck Veterinary Manual). Prevent: no fat does, more grain late in pregnancy, exercise (p. 291)
Enterotoxemia Found dead after a sudden change to grain, lush grass or lots of milk Treatment rarely works. Vaccinate (CD&T) pregnant does, and kids at 1 month with a booster (pp. 213–214)
Bloat Belly swollen, worst on the left; pain, after a feast of fresh green feed Raise the front feet on a block; drench a cup of vegetable oil (Peace Corps, p. 417; drench bottle)

Worms and FAMACHA (UF VM271, pp. 2–3):

  1. Every 2–4 weeks, score the inner eyelid against the FAMACHA card (1 = red, 5 = white), and check back (body condition), rear (scours), jaw (bottle jaw) and nose (pale eyelid and bottle jaw, in sheep).
  2. Deworm only the goats that need it. 20% of goats carry 80% of the worms; treating all of them breeds resistant worms.
  3. 80% of larvae live in the bottom 2 inches of forage: don't graze short. Rest pastures at least 30 days (it helps, but barber pole worm larvae can survive 3–6 months on pasture, so resting alone won't clear them).
  4. Goats browsing brush at head height pick up far fewer worms (rule of thumb).

Hooves: trim every 4–6 weeks, wall level with the sole (hoof parts; trimming). Wet winters bring hoof rot (photo; smelly, contagious): dry pens, a 15–18% zinc sulfate foot bath, clean trimmers (MSU M2487, p. 1).

🥛 Clean milk: milking, cooling, pasteurizing

Milking (Lincoln Univ, pp. 1–2; milking stand):

  1. Clip the udder hair. Squirt the first milk into a strip cup and look for clots every milking.
  2. Dip the teats; wipe each goat with her own towel. Milk without wringing the teats; dip again after.
  3. Filter, and chill to 38 °F within an hour. Cold raw milk keeps 12–14 days.

Cooling without power (USDA FB 976, pp. 6, 15; cooling tank): a can of 85 °F milk set in 3 times its volume of 37 °F water only gets to about 50 °F — use more water and stir. Spring water is often as warm as 65 °F; check it with a thermometer. In our 95 °F summers, milk you can't chill should become yogurt, cheese or ghee the same day (rule of thumb).

Why pasteurize: raw milk and soft raw-milk cheeses spread brucellosis, TB and Listeria; hard, aged cheeses are safer (Zoonoses, p. 92). Young children should not get raw milk; yogurt resists contamination better (WHO, p. 16). Plant poisons pass into milk too — lupine can cause birth defects in people (WSU, p. 15).

Pasteurizing at home (UMN, p. 1):

  1. Use a double boiler, or a pot set in a pan of water, so the milk doesn't scorch.
  2. Heat to 145 °F and hold 30 minutes, stirring, or to 161 °F for 15 seconds. If it drops below 145 °F, restart the 30 minutes (rule of thumb). UMN's own home method has a safety margin: stir until an accurate thermometer reads 165 °F, then cool at once.
  3. Cool it at once in cold running water or ice water. Kept cold, it lasts 7–10 days.
  4. No thermometer? Bring it just to a boil (rule of thumb).

🧈 Cream, butter and ghee

Goat cream barely rises, and goat butter is pure white (FB 920, p. 7), so sour the whole milk and churn it. Cow milk can be skimmed from a shallow pan 4 inches deep after 36 hours (ILCA, p. 38; drawing).

Butter from soured whole milk (ILCA, p. 60):

  1. Let the milk sour until it tastes pleasantly sour. Fresh milk churns slowly and loses fat.
  2. At 59–79 °F, rock or shake it in a closed vessel until butter grains form (small churns). Colder can take 5 hours.

Butter from cream (USDA FB 876, p. 23): ripen it at 65–75 °F until mildly sour, then churn at 52–66 °F for 30–40 minutes until the grains are wheat-sized. Drain the buttermilk (photo) and wash the butter twice. Salted to 3% (about ½ oz per pound (our arithmetic)), butter keeps up to 4 months sealed, cool and dark (ILCA, p. 49).

Ghee keeps a year without a fridge (Practical Action, p. 4):

  1. Boil butter gently until the water is gone and the milk solids turn golden brown.
  2. Let it settle; strain through boiled cheesecloth.
  3. Store airtight, dark and cool — not in iron or copper, which speed rancidity. It keeps more than 12 months at room temperature.

🧀 Yogurt, kefir and cheese

Yogurt (Practical Action, pp. 1–3):

  1. Heat milk to 176–185 °F and hold 15–20 minutes, stirring. Cool fast to 104–113 °F.
  2. Stir in 20–50 g of live yogurt per liter (about 1½–3 tablespoons per quart (our conversion)).
  3. Keep warm 4–6 hours — a haybox or a cooler with jars of hot water works — then chill.
  4. Keep a "mother culture" going; replace it if flavors go off.

Kefir needs no warm box (ILCA, p. 59): heat milk to 185 °F for 30 minutes and cool it, add kefir grains, and leave about 12 hours at room temperature (72 °F). Reuse the grains; they multiply (rule of thumb).

Lemon or vinegar cheese (queso blanco, paneer) (ILCA, p. 50; step chart):

  1. Heat milk to 180 °F, or a simmer.
  2. Stir in lemon juice, about 3 mL per 100 mL of milk (about 2 tablespoons per quart (our conversion)), diluted with equal water — or vinegar. Curds form at once. Settle 15 minutes.
  3. Drain in a cloth, salt, press overnight. Keeps about 3 days cold, 5 in brine (Practical Action, p. 1).

Cottage cheese with no bought culture (USDA FB 1451, pp. 6–7):

  1. Let skim milk clabber at about 75 °F (about 30 hours) until clean and pleasantly sour.
  2. Cut it in 2-inch pieces; set the pan in hot water to bring it to 100 °F; hold about 30 minutes, stirring gently. Drain in a cloth, rinse cold, salt. A gallon makes about 1½ lb.
  3. Homemade starter: set several boiled pint jars of fresh milk at 75 °F (starter jars). Keep the one that curdles in about 30 hours with a smooth curd and a clean, sharp sour taste, and carry it on daily.

A simple rennet cheese (FB 920, p. 8): stir a junket tablet dissolved in cold water into 1 gallon of milk at 88 °F. Cut the curd after 30 minutes, warm it to 98 °F, bag and press it (homemade lever press), and rub with salt for several days. Ready at 10 days.

Making your own rennet (Book of Cheese, pp. 55–56):

  1. Take the stomach of a young kid, lamb or calf that has had nothing but milk. Clean it, blow it up, dry it.
  2. Cut it open and soak it in 3–5% salt water (about 4–7 oz salt per gallon (our arithmetic)) for days to weeks.
  3. Filter, add more salt, and keep it cool and dark (stone jugs, colored glass) — light destroys it.
  4. Strength varies and it can spoil: test it on a small batch.

Aging (FB 1191, p. 15): a cellar at 50–60 °F is ideal. Turn cheeses daily and wipe off mold; coat them at 2 weeks (beeswax works); eat from 6 weeks. Cover cellar windows with cheesecloth — the cheese-skipper fly gets through screen.

Whey (Book of Cheese, p. 319): heat it to about 200 °F and skim the curds that float — ricotta. Boil the rest down to sweet brown mysost. Whey also feeds hens and pigs (rule of thumb), but never feed raw milk to pigs — it spreads TB (Zoonoses, p. 27).

🐝 Bees: hives, forage, swarms, feeding

Top-bar hive Langstroth hive
Building From scrap; only the bar width must be exact; a strip of beeswax on each bar guides the comb Precise carpentry and bought foundation
Harvest Cut comb: honey and wax with no extractor; combs are fragile Pays off with an extractor — empty combs go back in. A full deep frame holds about 5 lb
Source FAO, pp. 44–45 AH 335, pp. 72–73; plans; cutaway

Forage and the summer dearth (AH 335, p. 21 lists western bee plants). Here (rule of thumb): manzanita and willow bloom February–March, bigleaf maple in April, and blackberry in June–July is the main flow. A dearth follows from late July to the fall rains, when robbing and yellowjackets peak: reduce entrances (entrance reducer), never spill syrup or leave comb out (OSU EM 9186, p. 4), and keep water within half a mile (rule of thumb).

Swarms are free bees:

  • They come mostly March–June: the old queen and half the bees hang in a cluster for an hour or so (AH 335, p. 38; resting swarm). Swarming bees are full of honey and gentle (Langstroth, p. 41).
  • Shake the cluster into a box of old comb (hiving a swarm); close it at dusk. A bait hive of old dark comb catches swarms by itself (rule of thumb).
  • Your own colony swarms when it has more than 6 frames of bees and is crowded: split it when swarm cells appear (OSU EM 9186, p. 11; swarm cells).
  • Package bees from California will stop after a collapse, so swarms will be the only new bees (p. 14).

Feeding (AH 335, pp. 49–51): 1:1 sugar syrup in spring; thick 2:1 for new colonies and fall stores; dry sugar on the inner cover in a late-winter emergency. Feed late in the day with narrowed entrances. Never feed honey from unknown hives — it spreads disease. A broodless colony eats about 4 lb of honey a month (p. 72); the book's 90-lb winter store is for cold climates, and here plan on about 60 lb: an average colony eats about that between the fall and spring flows (p. 72), and our long summer dearth eats into stores too (rule of thumb).

🦠 Bee health: varroa and foulbrood

Varroa mites are the main killer; unchecked they double every month (HBHC, p. 5; mites on bees and larvae). Here they peak in August–September, as the winter bees are raised (rule of thumb).

Counting mites (HBHC, pp. 9–10):

  1. Shake bees from 1–3 brood frames (not the one with the queen) into a tub.
  2. Scoop ½ cup (about 300 bees) into a jar of rubbing alcohol or soapy water, shake, and count the mites.
  3. Mites ÷ 300 × 100 = mites per 100 bees. Treat at 1+ in winter and spring, 2+ in summer and fall.

Controls with nothing bought (pp. 39–40): cut out capped drone brood 2–3 times in spring, or force a brood break by caging the queen 1–2 weeks or making a split.

Oxalic acid dribble (p. 29): 35 g, weighed (roughly 2⅓ level tablespoons if you have no scale (rule of thumb)) in 1 liter of 1:1 syrup; 5 mL on the bees in each occupied gap between combs, 50 mL at most per colony, best with no capped brood (late fall, winter). Corrosive: gloves and goggles. Vaporizing it is extremely dangerous. Stockpile pure oxalic acid dihydrate now (rule of thumb).

American foulbrood (AFB) — the disease that means burning:

  • Signs: spotty brood; sunken, darker, pierced cappings; foul smell (FAO 2006, p. 10; dead larvae in cells).
  • Matchstick test: push a matchstick into a dead larva and draw it out slowly. About an inch of ropy brown goo means AFB (FAO Forest, p. 150; photo).
  • Then: burn combs, frames, honey and dead bees in a pit about 3 ft deep and fill it in; scorch the inside of the boxes until slightly brown. Shaking the bees onto clean new combs can save them (FAO 2006, p. 11).

🍯 Honey, wax, bears and stings

Honey with no extractor (FAO Forest, p. 91; Practical Action, p. 3): take only sealed comb and leave winter stores. Cut it as comb honey, or crush it and strain through coarse, then fine, cloth. Keep everything bone-dry — honey over 19–20% water ferments. Don't heat it; crystallized honey is fine.

Beeswax (FAO Forest, pp. 112–113): melt comb in water, strain through sacking, and let the wax cake float and set; repeat through finer cloth. A solar melter (a black box with a glass lid) also works. Wax is highly flammable — never heat it dry over a flame (double boiler). Beeswax candles need a thicker wick than tallow (Practical Action, p. 2).

Bear fence (UF ENY-2060, pp. 1–5): bears come for the brood, and come back. Run three electric wires at 8, 20 and 32 inches on heavy T-posts every 8 ft, at least 3 ft from the hives, with a 6-ft ground rod (step photos; finished fence). Test for 6,000–8,000 volts and keep plants cut back 2–3 ft. Bait the wire with foil smeared with bacon grease so a bear's nose gets the first shock (rule of thumb).

Stings: pain and swelling at the spot are normal; scrape the stinger out at once (rule of thumb). A whole-body reaction (anaphylaxis: throat swelling, trouble breathing, faintness) follows 0.3–7.5% of stings (OSU EM 9186, p. 4).

  • Give epinephrine (adrenaline) into the thigh muscle: adults 50 kg+ 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000), repeated every 5 minutes as needed; children 0.15 mg (WHO, p. 235). See the Field Care guide.

⚠️ Old advice in the library to ignore

  • Phenothiazine mixed into salt as a constant dewormer — FB 920, p. 26. Breeds resistant worms. Instead: FAMACHA-targeted deworming.
  • Iodoform in mineral oil put into the uterus — FB 920, p. 30. Outdated. Instead: never pull the afterbirth (KSU, p. 13).
  • Caustic-stick disbudding — FB 920, p. 34. It runs into eyes and burns other kids or the doe. Instead: a hot iron.
  • Pasteurizing at 140 °F (60 °C) for 30 minutes — Zoonoses, p. 31. Too low. Instead: 145 °F for 30 minutes or 161 °F for 15 seconds.
  • Paraffin heated to 240 °F for dipping cheese — FB 1191, p. 15 (220 °F in Book of Cheese, p. 287). Fire and burn hazard. Instead: melt wax in a pot set in water, never unattended.
  • Soft cheese from raw milk — Book of Cheese, p. 167; FB 920, p. 8. It carries Listeria and brucellosis. Instead: pasteurize milk for soft cheese.
  • Bee drugs — AH 335, p. 132. The 1980 book predates varroa in the US (1987). Instead: the HBHC varroa guide.
  • Killing colonies over burning sulfur to take the honey — Langstroth, p. 264. Instead: take only surplus honey. Burn a colony only for AFB.
  • Butter coloring and old dairy "preservatives" — FB 876, p. 13. Instead: salt to 3%, or make ghee.
