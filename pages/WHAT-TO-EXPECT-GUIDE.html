⏳ What to Expect: Disaster Scenarios & Timelines

What really happens, hour by hour and month by month, in the disasters most likely to hit the Illinois Valley, and what to do at each stage. The timelines come from official plans and after-action reports: the Oregon Resilience Plan, the Josephine County hazard plan, the state's review of the 2020 Labor Day fires, federal and state power-outage and fuel plans, and fifty years of disaster research. The books are in Disaster-Scenarios\ (Cascadia-Earthquake, Local-Hazards, Fire-Heat-Drought, Grid-Supply-Pandemic, How-People-Respond), with more in Wildfire\, Earthquake-Tsunami\, Nuclear-Radiation\ and EMP-Solar-Storms\.

⚠️ Five rules that hold in every scenario

  1. Plan to be on your own for at least two weeks. The state's own Cascadia exercise said so (Cascadia Rising, p. 12). At the end of one highway, plan longer.
  2. Leave early. Alerts fail when cell towers burn or lose power (2020 fire review, p. 9). A Level 2 "Be Set" notice may be the only warning you get.
  3. Check on people who live alone. Most who died in the 2021 heat dome lived alone (heat report, p. 10).
  4. Generators, grills and camp stoves stay outdoors. Carbon monoxide kills in every outage (CPSC).
  5. Expect neighbors to help, and be one. Panic and looting are rare; survivors do most of the rescuing (Quarantelli, p. 16).

  1. Find your scenario in the table below, or the one closest to what is happening.
  2. Read its timeline for the stage you are in: what is happening around you, and what to do.
  3. Do its "Get ready now" list while things are normal. Most items point to another guide.
  4. Read What every scenario has in common once. It applies to all of them.

🔥 1. Wildfire and smoke

What it is and how likely here. Fires start here every summer: 1,175 in 2010–2019, about 80% started by people (county plan, pp. 72–73). Burn probability is "considerably higher along highway 199 between Grants Pass and Cave Junction," so a fire could cut the valley off (p. 71). In September 2020 the Slater and Devil fires burned over 166,000 acres and closed highways around the county (p. 73), while east winds spread fires statewide: over 500,000 Oregonians were under some level of evacuation notice (state review, p. 20).

Warning you'll get: days for the weather (the Weather Service warned of the Labor Day winds ahead of time); minutes to hours for a fire near you.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Fire runs with the wind; embers start new fires ahead of it. Levels 1–2–3 go out, but some cell towers burned in 2020 and alerts failed (p. 9). Power cut or lines down. Roads jam. Go at Level 2 if you can; at Level 3 go now. Two routes out. N95 masks. Move livestock early, in daylight.
First 3 days Shelters open (in 2020 the Red Cross ran 15, with almost 2,000 people — p. 20). US-199 may close. Thick smoke. No power means no well water. Stay out until allowed back. Keep the sick, old and young in a cleaner-air room. Check in with family by radio or text.
1–2 weeks Big fires can burn for weeks (estimate); smoke comes and goes. Re-entry begins; burned poles and lines are replaced; weakened trees fall. Shock-chlorinate and test the well before drinking (OWRD). N95, gloves and boots in ash.
1–3 months Fall rains usually end the season (rule of thumb). Burned slopes can let go as debris flows in the first big storms (FEMA, p. 107). Watch burned slopes above houses and roads when it pours. Help neighbors clear debris. Plan winter shelter if a house is lost.
3–12 months Debris removal and rebuilding; housing is scarce. Rebuild with a fire-resistant roof, ember-proof vents and a 5-ft clear zone.
1 year+ "Recovery efforts will be on-going for many years" (p. 14). Redo defensible space every spring.

Signs it's getting worse: dry east wind, humidity in the teens, a red flag warning, a smoke column building in the afternoon (Pyrocumulonimbus), ash falling, power shut off ahead of wind. Getting better: wind turns back from the west, humidity rises, rain, containment numbers climb.

❄️ 2. Winter storm, ice, flood and landslides

What it is and how likely here. The county rates winter storms #2 of all hazards: high probability, high vulnerability. Recent ones: in February 2019 fallen trees closed US-199 at the California line and 2,500 customers lost power around Grants Pass, Cave Junction and O'Brien; in January 2021 snow left 45,000 customers in the area without power (county plan, p. 64). The valley is "one way in, one way out": a storm on US-199 can strand Cave Junction and cut the whole valley's power "for an extended period" (p. 140). Floods are rated high probability, moderate damage. The benchmark is the Christmas flood of 1964: 10 inches of rain in six days, the Rogue and Illinois Valleys cut off and I-5 closed (p. 45). In 2015, water covered US-199 near Selma and closed roads near O'Brien (p. 46).

Warning you'll get: usually days (forecasts of snow, ice or an atmospheric river); minutes for a flash flood or landslide.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Trees and lines come down; roads blocked; creeks rise fast. Stay off the roads. Never drive into flooded roads (FEMA, p. 54). Get water stored before the pump stops.
First 3 days Power out across the valley; phones fade as tower batteries run down (outage annex, p. 107). Stores and stations closed or empty. Heat one room. Generator outdoors only. Check on neighbors, especially older people alone.
1–2 weeks Most past big-storm outages were fixed within about two weeks (outage annex, p. 10); far-out rural lines come last (estimate). Slides close roads. Ration fuel and firewood. Watch slopes for warning signs (below).
1–3 months Landslide and washout repairs; some roads one lane or closed. Plan longer trips around closures; keep a spare week of supplies.
3–12 months Bigger slides on highways can take many months (estimate). Fix drainage above the house before next winter.
1 year+ Back to normal for most. Restock.

Signs it's getting worse: freezing rain (ice on lines and trees), a second storm forecast behind the first, rising creeks at night. Landslide warning signs: doors or windows that suddenly stick, new cracks in walls or ground, tilting trees or poles, water bursting from new places, a rumble that gets louder (FEMA, p. 108; Landslide). Getting better: temperatures above freezing, rivers falling, crews working on the main lines.

Get ready now

🌡️ 3. Extreme heat and drought

What it is and how likely here. The county rates heat waves high probability, moderate damage. Days with a heat index over 90 °F are expected to rise from about 13 a year to about 40 by the 2050s (county plan, p. 57). In the June 2021 heat dome Josephine County saw 92–108 °F, with two more heat waves later that summer (p. 58). In Portland it killed 72 people; in a normal year, none. Most were 60 or older and lived alone, and half had only a fan (heat report, pp. 2, 8–12). Drought is also rated high probability: extreme drought years 1924, 1977 and 2001, severe ones as recently as 2014 and 2020 (p. 33).

Warning you'll get: days for heat; months for drought (a dry winter and low snow).

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Afternoons over 100 °F; houses don't cool at night. Fire danger jumps; power may be shut off for fire risk. Cool one room (shade, wet cloths, a basement). Above the upper 90s a fan only moves hot air (heat report, p. 12).
First 3 days Deaths peak after the hottest day — in Portland the worst day was the day after 116 °F (heat report, p. 7). Visit or call anyone living alone twice a day. Do outdoor work at dawn (rule of thumb).
1–2 weeks Heat breaks, then can return; Josephine had three heat waves in summer 2021. Creeks and shallow wells drop. Cut water use; shade gardens and animals.
1–3 months Drought: wells yield less in late summer (OWRD); pastures brown early; fire season long. Water the garden at the roots; save seed from survivors.
3–12 months Short harvests; hay expensive or gone (estimate). Plan feed and food for winter early.
1 year+ Droughts can run for years: severe or extreme in 1924, 1926, 1931 and 1934 (p. 33). Store rainwater; plant drought-tough crops.

Signs it's getting worse: night lows staying in the 70s, a forecast of more days over 100 °F, confusion or no sweating in someone hot (Heat stroke — an emergency). Getting better: marine air and cooler nights; fall rains.

🔌 4. Long power outage, including fire-season shutoffs

What it is and how likely here. Storms knock out power almost every winter (scenario 2). Pacific Power has mapped areas near Cave Junction where it may cut power on purpose in dangerous fire weather, called a public safety power shutoff (county plan, p. 140; state fire review, p. 10). Longer outages come with bigger disasters: about two weeks after Hurricane Sandy and Katrina in most places, 11 days in Connecticut in 2011 (outage annex, p. 10), 1–3 months after a Cascadia quake (Resilience Plan, p. 22), months to years after a grid-wrecking solar storm or EMP (scenario 9).

Warning you'll get: shutoffs are usually announced ahead by the utility (verify with Pacific Power); storm outages give hours; quake or EMP none.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Well pumps, furnaces, fridges stop. Phones and towers run on batteries or generators for "hours to a few days" (outage annex, p. 107). Fill containers if any pressure is left. Keep fridge and freezer shut.
First 3 days Gas stations can't pump without power, as after Hurricane Sandy (NASEO, p. 72); stores close. Septic pumps stop (Texas AgriLife). Cell service fades. Cook the thawing food first. Generator outdoors, through a transfer switch only. AM radio for news.
1–2 weeks Most storm outages end here. Fuel for generators runs short. Run the generator a few hours a day. Bucket toilet if the septic pump is out.
1–3 months Cascadia-scale outage. Water systems, clinics and stations with no fuel shut down (estimate). Off-grid power for the essentials; preserve food without a freezer.
3–12 months A big transformer takes about a year to replace (Meta-R-319, p. 149). Shift to wood heat, hand pumps, root cellar, daylight hours.
1 year+ Only after EMP or a huge solar storm (estimate). Long-term off-grid living — this library.

Signs it's getting worse: no restoration estimate from the utility, damage to big lines or substations, fuel shortages. Getting better: crews in the area, power back in town first.

Get ready now

🦠 5. Pandemic

What it is and how likely here. A new germ spreads around the world, usually "within several weeks or months." The last century had flu pandemics in 1918, 1957, 1968 and 2009 (WHO, pp. 51–52), and then COVID-19 — so expect one or two in a lifetime (estimate). A pandemic comes in waves of several weeks to a few months, and a later wave can be worse: the mild spring wave of 1918 came before the deadly autumn one (p. 52; Spanish flu).

Warning you'll get: weeks to months from the first news to cases here.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours (Weeks of news first.) Panic buying can empty shelves even when supply is fine, as after Japan's 2011 quake (CNA, p. 41). Buy steadily, not in a panic; fill gaps in medicines and soap.
First 3 days Cases appear locally; schools may close; rumors spread. Plan a sickroom; decide how the household will isolate a sick person.
1–2 weeks The first wave builds. More people miss work than are sick (WHO, p. 51); school closures alone may keep about 16% home (p. 52). Clinics are crowded. Keep sick people apart; wash hands; nurse at home with fluids.
1–3 months A wave peaks and falls; early waves use up masks and medicines (p. 51). Restock between waves. Teach children at home.
3–12 months More waves, of different sizes. Don't let your guard down after the first wave.
1 year+ The "transition" phase: the risk falls and emergency measures wind down (WHO, p. 14). Repair what was put off.

Signs it's getting worse: sick people in more than one household nearby, health workers falling ill, shortages of medicines. Getting better: fewer new cases week after week, schools reopening.

Get ready now

⛽ 6. Economic crisis, fuel and supply-chain breakdown

What it is and how likely here. Oregon has no refineries; more than 90% of its fuel comes from four refineries near Seattle, mostly through one pipeline to the Portland fuel hub (fuel plan, p. 9). Gas stations hold about two to three days of fuel (p. 11). Groceries hold more shelf-stable food than most people think; the real problem in a catastrophe is delivery, not supply (CNA, p. 9). In the 1970s oil shortages states used odd-even days to cut gas lines (NASEO, p. 45; 1973 oil crisis). In the Great Depression people formed barter associations to trade work and goods (Braun 1933, p. 5).

Warning you'll get: days to months — prices rise, then shortages begin.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours News of a shortage starts a run on fuel and food (panic buying); even a "don't top off your tank" message does (fuel plan, p. 87). Don't join the run if you're stocked; top up slowly beforehand.
First 3 days Stations run dry; shelves empty of staples; cash only if card systems fail (estimate). Keep some cash in small bills. Stay home; save fuel.
1–2 weeks Rationing: odd/even days, purchase limits (fuel plan, p. 54). Combine trips; carpool; bike.
1–3 months Jobs and wages hit; neighbors start trading (estimate). Trade skills and surplus fairly; start a garden bed.
3–12 months A real depression: barter groups and local scrip appear, as in 1932–33 (Braun, p. 17). Organize a skills list and a barter day.
1 year+ A depression can last years (estimate). Grow, preserve and repair locally.

Signs it's getting worse: stations limiting gallons, stores limiting items, delivery trucks skipping the valley. Getting better: limits lifted, prices steady.

Get ready now

🌎 7. Cascadia M9 earthquake (and the coast tsunami)

What it is and how likely here. The Cascadia subduction zone last broke on January 26, 1700 (1700 Cascadia earthquake). About half of past quakes were centered off the southern Oregon coast; the odds of one hitting southern Oregon in the next 50 years are about 37% (Resilience Plan, p. 31). The county puts it at 37–43% for any Cascadia quake and 7–12% for the full M9, and rates our vulnerability high (county plan, p. 39). Studies estimate 650–5,000 deaths from the quake and another 600–5,000 from the tsunami (Resilience Plan, p. 40).

Warning you'll get: none. The shaking is the warning. It lasts 4–6 minutes (CREW, p. 10).

When What's happening (Valley zone unless noted) What you do
First minutes–hours Shaking 4–6 minutes; landslides on mountain roads; chimneys and older houses damaged (estimate). The tsunami reaches parts of the coast within 15 minutes (fuel plan, p. 5). Phones jammed or dead. Drop, cover, hold on. Then injuries and fires. Smell gas or propane? Get out first, then shut it off (Be 2 Weeks Ready, p. 80). Don't drive.
First 3 days Aftershocks, a few possibly over M7 (CREW, p. 13). No outside help. US-199 likely blocked. Stations' 2–3 days of fuel goes. Check every neighbor. Set up water, toilet and a cooking spot outdoors. Listen to AM radio and the ham nets.
1–2 weeks Oregon's fuel terminals hold only 3–5 days of fuel and sit on shaky ground (Resilience Plan, p. 318); fuel rationing. Coast towns are cut off and need shelter and food (p. 33); some people may come inland over US-199 when it opens (estimate). Organize the neighborhood: water, food sharing, a first-aid post. Plan how to take in or help coast refugees.
1–3 months Electricity back in 1–3 months in the valley zone (3–6 on the coast); police and fire stations 2–4 months (Resilience Plan, p. 22). Off-grid power, wood heat. Repair the house before winter.
3–12 months Water and sewer: 1 month to 1 year (coast 1–3 years). Top highways partly open: 6–12 months. Fuel system: months or longer (fuel plan, p. 5). A strong aftershock months later can do more damage (CREW, p. 13). Garden, preserve, barter. Expect hauling by bike, cart or horse.
1 year+ Hospitals: 18 months (coast 3 years or more). The plan warns of a possible "lost generation" of economic decline (p. 22). Long-term local living. Help rebuild.

Signs it's getting worse: fires spreading in town, a dam or bridge reported out, strong aftershocks, fuel gone. Getting better: a Grants Pass route open, radio reports of supply points, power back in town.

Get ready now

🚨 8. Riots and law and order breaking down

What it is and how likely here. Fifty years of disaster research found panic flight so rare that fire researchers stopped using the idea, and looting "unusual in the typical natural and technological disasters" of countries like ours (Quarantelli, pp. 6–7, 11–12). Mass looting did happen after Hurricane Hugo on St. Croix and after Hurricane Katrina, where three things came together: many poor people living beside obvious wealth, organized youth gangs, and a police force that was ineffective or corrupt (pp. 13–14). Even there, help between people "swamped" the theft, and after the first emergency days reported crime usually drops (p. 14). The EMP Commission warns that disorder becomes more likely when shortages cause conflict and police are absent for a long time (EMP Commission 2008, p. 167).

Warning you'll get: usually days to weeks of growing shortages or anger first.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Rumors travel faster than facts. Check rumors with someone who saw it. Stay home and calm.
First 3 days In most disasters crime falls; neighbors help. Be visible helping; know who on your road is alone.
1–2 weeks If supply and police fail together: theft of fuel, food and tools (estimate). Lock up fuel and tools; keep a low profile with supplies.
1–3 months Neighborhood watch, shared patrols, local agreements (estimate). Join or start a watch group; settle disputes early and fairly.
3–12 months Communities that organized recover order first (estimate). Support a fair local council and rules.
1 year+ Order rebuilds from local groups up (estimate). Keep records of agreements and trades.

Signs it's getting worse: police no longer answering, groups moving road to road, threats over fuel or water. Getting better: regular patrols, markets reopening, people out in daylight.

Get ready now

☀️ 9. Solar storm or EMP

What it is and how likely here. A severe geomagnetic storm or a nuclear burst high above the country (EMP) mainly threatens the power grid. NOAA counts about four extreme (G5) storms per 11-year solar cycle (NOAA scales, p. 1), but most do not wreck grids; a Carrington Event-size storm is rare (estimate). Details, tests and protecting gear: EMP guide.

Warning you'll get: solar storm 18–72 hours, with a 20–45 minute final warning (CISA, p. 21); EMP none.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Grid may collapse (Quebec's went down in 90 seconds in 1989 — Meta-R-319, p. 149); GPS and HF radio unreliable. After an EMP some running cars stall and restart; parked cars were not affected in tests (EMP Commission 2008, p. 131). Unplug electronics; disconnect antennas; get the shielded spares out afterward.
First 3 days Like any long outage (scenario 4), across a whole region. Water, food, heat; don't waste fuel.
1–2 weeks No outside help coming from cities also in the dark. Organize the neighborhood.
1–3 months Big transformers damaged; each takes about a year to replace (Meta-R-319, p. 149). Off-grid power and hand tools.
3–12 months The EMP Commission expected months to years for recovery (EMP Commission, p. 27). Full local self-reliance.
1 year+ Slow rebuilding of the grid. Same.

Signs it's getting worse: auroras over Oregon (Aurora), a G4–G5 watch, widespread outage with no restoration estimate. Getting better: power back in Grants Pass.

Get ready now

☢️ 10. Nuclear attack and fallout

What it is and how likely here. A nuclear detonation somewhere upwind could drop fallout on the valley. No source on this drive gives honest odds; treat it as low but not zero (estimate). Everything about sheltering is in the Nuclear Fallout Guide.

Warning you'll get: minutes or none. A 3–5 minute wavering siren tone is the attack warning (FEMA H-20, p. 12). Fallout near a blast can arrive in 15–30 minutes; 100–200 miles downwind, often 5–10 hours or more (H-20, p. 7).

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Officials tell everyone within about 50 miles to shelter (Planning Guidance, p. 54). Fallout dust may begin to fall. Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Best shelter you can reach before fallout arrives.
First 3 days Radiation drops 90% in 7 hours and 99% in 2 days (H-20, p. 8). Stay sheltered at least 12–24 hours unless told otherwise (Planning Guidance, p. 53).
1–2 weeks In heavy fallout, shelter "several days to two weeks," with short trips out later (H-20, p. 9). Decontaminate on entry; stored and canned food is fine.
1–3 months Fresh milk unsafe while radioactive iodine decays (USDA says 30–60 days — PA-515, p. 8). Wash and peel garden produce; use stored milk for babies.
3–12 months Recovery depends on how many cities were hit; supply chains may be broken nationwide (estimate). See scenarios 4 and 6.
1 year+ Long-term living with shortages (estimate). This library.

Signs it's getting worse: a meter reading that is rising, fallout dust visible on cars. Getting better: readings falling by the 7-10 rule; officials saying it's clear.

Get ready now

🌋 11. Volcanic ash

What it is and how likely here. The nearest potentially active volcanoes are Crater Lake, Medicine Lake, Mount Shasta, Newberry and the Three Sisters. The chance of 0.4 inch or more of ash here is about 1 in 10,000 a year; the risks are ashfall, bad air and disruption (county plan, p. 68). Winds usually carry ash east of the Cascades, away from us (p. 68).

Warning you'll get: usually days to weeks of earthquakes and steam before a big eruption (rule of thumb); hours as an ash cloud drifts.

When What's happening What you do
First minutes–hours Sky darkens; gritty ash falls. Inside; windows shut. Goggles and a dust mask outside (FEMA, p. 104).
First 3 days Ash on roofs, roads and gardens; engines clog. Clear heavy ash from roofs and gutters (FEMA, p. 104); cover the well and water tanks.
1–2 weeks Cleanup; air filters and engines need changing. Wash produce; keep animals off ashy pasture if you can (rule of thumb).
1–3 months Mostly back to normal unless eruptions continue (estimate). Watch the radio for new eruptions.
3–12 months Ash washes into creeks and gardens with the rains (estimate). Settle and filter creek water before treating it.
1 year+ No lasting change expected from a thin ashfall (estimate). Restock masks and filters.

Signs it's getting worse / better: more ash forecasts, or skies clearing and rain washing it down.

Get ready now

🤝 What every scenario has in common

The first 72 hours. No outside help, so neighbors are the first responders. In the first half hour after a disaster, about a third of survivors search for missing people and about one in ten actively rescue them (Quarantelli, p. 16). Your jobs: injuries, fires, water, a toilet, warmth or cooling, and checking the people next door.

The 2-week mark. Oregon tells everyone to be ready for at least two weeks (Be 2 Weeks Ready, p. 2). The old 2004 FEMA book's "at least three days" (p. 33) is too short for here. Two weeks is also about when most big storm outages have ended, and the longest disruption most businesses can stand (Resilience Plan, p. 22).

When things usually improve. Most storm, fire and heat emergencies ease within days to weeks. A Cascadia quake, EMP or national crisis takes months to years (see the tables). Plan for the long tail, not just the first days.

Why most neighbors help. After disasters, help and sharing far outweigh theft, even in the worst cases (Quarantelli, p. 14); citizen groups spring up to clear debris, move supplies and pass messages (EMP Commission 2008, p. 167). Plan on cooperating, and organize early.

Rumor control. Rumors make things worse. Pick one trusted source (a radio station, the ham net, a neighborhood runner) and write down news with the time and who said it (rule of thumb); help correct misinformation (Psychological First Aid, p. 35).

Mental health. Feeling overwhelmed, confused, afraid or numb is a normal reaction (WHO Psychological First Aid, p. 9). Keep routines, give everyone a job, keep children close and talking, and watch for anyone who stops eating or caring for themselves (rule of thumb). How-to: Community & barter and Psychological first aid.

🔁 When disasters stack

Disasters often come together. Plan for the pairs most likely here (estimates, from the scenarios above):

Combination Why it is worse What to add
Cascadia quake + winter Power out 1–3 months with no heat; roads blocked by snow and slides. Firewood stacked for the whole winter; a warm room you can heat without power.
Wildfire + heat + power shutoff No fans or pump in 100 °F heat while you wait to evacuate; smoke keeps windows shut. Stored water, a cool room, a car ready to leave early.
Pandemic + fire or flood Crowded shelters spread illness — the 2020 fires had to shelter people during COVID-19 (state review, p. 29). Evacuate to friends or family instead of a shelter; carry masks and soap.
Pandemic + economic crisis Jobs lost while people are sick; supplies short. Deep food storage and barter skills.
Drought + wildfire Dry wells and dry forest in the same summer. Water storage and defensible space together.
Solar storm or EMP + winter Grid down for months in the cold. Wood heat, hand water pump, food that doesn't need a freezer.
