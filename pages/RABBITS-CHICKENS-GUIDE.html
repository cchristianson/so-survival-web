🐇🐔 Rabbits & Chickens for Meat and Eggs

How to keep meat rabbits and a self-renewing chicken flock with no feed store, vet or hatchery: breeds, hutches and coops, local predators, feeding on hay, greens, grain and scraps, breeding and kindling, hatching chicks under hens, eggs, summer heat, common diseases, slaughter and butchering, manure, and keeping the breeding lines going for years. The books are in Small-Livestock\ (Rabbits, Chickens, Slaughter-Processing) and Animals\ (Poultry, Rabbits-Other, Veterinary, Planning-Predators-Toxic-Plants).

⚠️ Rules that prevent the worst losses

  • Lock birds in every night behind hardware cloth, not chicken wire, buried at least 12 inches. A weasel kills several birds at once and fits through a ¼-inch gap (Kentucky ID-245, pp. 3, 6).
  • Heat kills rabbits. Plan litters for spring and fall, not July–August, and have shade, airflow and water ready before 100 °F days (USDA FB 1730, p. 31).
  • Never feed heated, moldy or sprayed greens, or plants from where dogs and cats go. Scotch broom and foxglove — everywhere here — are toxic (WSNWCB, p. 55).
  • Keep breeds that can renew themselves. Hybrid layers and Cornish-cross broilers rarely go broody and won't rebuild a flock (FAO, p. 10).
  • Rabbit meat alone is not a diet — it is very lean. Eat it with fat and starch.
  • The old bulletins' medicines are poisons (sodium fluoride, carbon tetrachloride, nicotine, DDT, lindane). See the last section.

🐇 Choosing breeds and starting stock

Rabbits:

  • New Zealand or Californian — by far the leading meat breeds, for steady color and growth (meat breeds). Most meat rabbits are butchered by 10 weeks (OSU EC 1651, p. 1).
  • Start with 2–3 does per buck (6 does and 2 bucks is typical). Buy from several different breeders to avoid inbreeding. Pick thick-boned rabbits, as deep as they are wide (EC 1651, p. 2; body shape to look for).

Chickens (USDA FB 1508, p. 6; FB 1363, p. 8):

Breed Good for Notes
Plymouth Rock, Rhode Island Red, Wyandotte, Orpington, Australorp Eggs and meat Most make good mothers — the best choice for a self-renewing flock (Buff Orpington)
Leghorn, Ancona, Minorca Eggs Fly over fences; "fickle mothers" (Leghorn flock)
Brahma, Jersey Giant, Cochin Meat Sit well but are clumsy on eggs
Commercial hybrids, Cornish-cross Short-term only "Very low mothering ability and broodiness" (FAO, p. 10)

🏠 Hutches and coops

Rabbit hutches (USDA AIB 358, pp. 8–10; OSU EC 1654, p. 2; two-compartment hutch; building plans):

  • About 2 ft high, no more than 2½ ft deep; 3 ft long for small breeds, 4 ft for medium, 6 ft for large. At least ¾ sq ft per pound of rabbit.
  • Scrap lumber and hardware cloth work. Give each hutch shade, a rain-proof roof and no drafts.
  • Water bottles or nipples, not crocks (crocks get fouled). No cedar shavings.
  • A resting board on part of a wire floor prevents sore hocks (R-41, p. 26).

Colony or ground pens (rule of thumb): they save wire and work, but rabbits dig out (bury mesh or a 2-ft wire apron; cover the top), catch coccidiosis and tapeworm from soil, and you lose track of matings. Does in heat ride each other and conceive less (R-41, p. 41); extra bucks fight — castrate or remove them (p. 67). Keep breeding does in their own hutches; put only growing fryers on grass in movable pens.

Chicken coops (OSU EC 1644, pp. 1–2; coop photo):

  • 3 sq ft per bird in the coop with a run, 8–10 sq ft without one.
  • Raise the coop 1 ft off the ground. Roosts 2 ft high, 5–10 inches of pole per bird (roost layouts).
  • Run of 1×2-inch mesh, top covered, fence buried 6–12 inches (Kentucky says 12+).
  • A movable pen (a chicken tractor) puts birds on fresh grass and breaks the parasite cycle.

📚 Go deeper: USDA AIB 358, p. 10 — scrap-lumber hutch plans; OSU EC 1654, p. 1 — cages, drafts and bedding; OSU EC 1644 — coop design for Oregon.

🦝 Predators of the Illinois Valley

Who did it? (Kentucky ID-245, pp. 2–3) — all of these live here:

Predator What you find
Bobcat Whole bird eaten or gone; leftovers covered with leaves and grass (coyotes and foxes don't do this; here a cougar also covers its kills, usually bigger ones like goats or deer (general practice))
Raccoon Head, crop and breast eaten; heads pulled through the wire; eggs carried 50+ yards
Fox Bird gone; a few drops of blood and feathers
Opossum Feeding started at the vent
Weasel or mink Several birds killed, bloody, little eaten. A least weasel fits a ¼-inch hole
Hawk or owl Daytime (hawk) or night (owl) losses from open runs

Keeping them out (ID-245, pp. 5–6; photos):

  1. Hardware cloth, buried at least 12 inches. Chicken wire keeps chickens in, not raccoons out.
  2. Lock birds in every night. Feed them inside at dusk and they learn to go in on their own.
  3. Cover the run against hawks and owls, and remove perches within 100 yards. Hawks and owls are federally protected — don't shoot them.
  4. A guardian dog (such as a Great Pyrenees) that stays with the flock works day and night (raising one).
  5. Rabbits too: hawks swoop into open pens; dogs and raccoons bend wire and open simple latches (OSU EC 1654, p. 1) — use clips a paw can't work (rule of thumb).

Big predators: for cougars, fence and shelter stock and shut them in sheds at night; Oregon law lets a landowner kill a cougar that is damaging livestock (ODFW, p. 2). For black bears, a properly installed electric fence is the most reliable protection for a coop (ODFW, p. 2; building a bear fence, step by step (beehive version)).

📚 Go deeper: Kentucky ID-245, p. 2 — predator signs and fixes in detail; ODFW Raising Chickens in Bear Country — electric fence setup; USDA Livestock Guarding Dogs, p. 13 — raising a guardian pup.

🌾 Feeding on little bought feed

Rabbits without pellets (USDA AIB 358, p. 12):

  • Leafy legume hay (alfalfa, clover, vetch) + whole grain (oats, wheat, barley, rye, sorghum, buckwheat, soft corn) + salt.
  • Roots all year, especially winter: carrots, turnips, beets, mangels, sweet potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes (FB 1730, p. 11).
  • Expect 3–9 oz of feed per rabbit a day, and slower growth than on pellets — home mixes are hard to balance (OSU EC 1655, p. 1).

Feeding greens safely (FB 1730, p. 12; R-41, pp. 37–38):

  1. Fresh, once a day, only what they eat in 4–5 minutes, in a manger, never on the floor.
  2. Dew-wet greens are fine. Greens that sat in a pile and heated are dangerous.
  3. Never forage from where dogs or cats defecate (tapeworm, coccidiosis), or that was sprayed.
  4. Change the diet slowly. A big feed of lush greens can kill rabbits used to pellets.

Chickens on scraps, greens and range:

  • Mix each day's table scraps into a grain mash. Give greens all the time (clippings, cabbage, lettuce). No onion tops, geranium leaves or strong-flavored scraps — they taint eggs (FB 1508, pp. 26–27). Scraps are a supplement: too many lower laying (PNW 477, p. 4).
  • Winter greens: sprouted oats. Soak oats 24 hours in a pail with small holes in the bottom, keep moist and rinse daily until they sprout (FB 1508, p. 27).
  • Foragers eat insects, worms, snails, seeds, grass and leaves; village hens' crops held 72% household waste (FAO, pp. 17–18). You can grow fly maggots on wet seed husks or dung (p. 24) — black soldier fly larvae are a common choice (rule of thumb).
  • With a dairy goat or cow: layers can live on skim milk or buttermilk to drink, all the grain they want, and oyster shell — about 4–5 gallons of milk a day per 100 hens (FB 1841, pp. 23–24).
  • Calcium always: oyster shell, limestone or crushed baked eggshell. The flock's own shells aren't enough, and short hens start eating their eggs (Practical Poultry Raising, p. 117). Grit or gravel for confined birds (FB 1508, p. 28).

Our dry season: scavenged feed dries up (FAO, p. 36) — here, roughly July–October when the grass browns. Store hay, grain, squash and roots for summer as well as winter (rule of thumb).

Toxic plants: rhododendron, nightshades, foxglove, Scotch broom, poison oak, tansy, black locust, false hellebore, snowberry, clematis (WSNWCB, p. 55). Penned birds are at more risk — they eat what they're given (p. 54). Also never feed moldy feed, avocado, raw dried beans, green or sprouted potatoes, or rhubarb leaves (rule of thumb).

Water: a doe and her 8-week litter of seven drink about a gallon a day in summer (FB 1730, p. 12). Fresh water for chickens every day (FB 1508, p. 28; homemade waterers). A hen drinks about a pint a day, double in heat, and a day without water can stop laying for weeks (rule of thumb).

🐣 Breeding rabbits and kindling

Step When Source
First breeding Small breeds 4–6 months, medium 7–9, large 9–11 EC 1655, p. 1
Mating Always take the doe to the buck's hutch same
Gestation 28–35 days, 31 on average EC 1655, p. 2
Nest box in Day 27 after mating AIB 358, p. 16; nest-box plans
Weaning Leave young with the doe until 8 weeks AIB 358, p. 18
Litters a year Four possible if none fail FB 1730, p. 19

Kindling:

  1. Write the mating date on the hutch. Put in the nest box with clean straw on day 27. Without one, kits born on the wire can die (EC 1655, p. 2).
  2. A day or two before kindling the doe pulls fur to line the nest — normal (AIB 358, p. 16; litter in a fur-lined nest).
  3. Next day, while the doe is busy eating, check the nest quietly and remove any dead kits (rule of thumb).
  4. Time it for our climate: breed so litters come in spring and fall, not July–August.

If this goes wrong (rule of thumb): doe won't take the buck → try again in a few days or another buck. Kits cold on the wire → warm them in your hands and return them to the nest. Born outside the box → move kits and fur into it.

🐤 Hatching and raising chicks

With a broody hen — the dependable way without power (USDA FB 1363):

  1. Broody hens cluck and ruffle up when approached. After she has stayed on the nest 2–3 days, move her at night to a quiet nest with dummy eggs (p. 9).
  2. Set 10–13 eggs in cold weather, 13–15 in warm (p. 3). Use fresh eggs — quality drops after 5 days, and 3-week-old eggs rarely hatch (p. 5).
  3. Candle on day 7 and day 14 (hold each egg to a bright light in the dark) and remove clear ones (p. 18; the air cell on days 7, 14 and 18; candling).
  4. Chicks start pipping the evening of day 19 and are out by day 21 (FB 1538, p. 11). Don't help them out — those that can't hatch alone rarely thrive (p. 15).

Incubators (only if you have steady power) (FB 1538, pp. 11, 16): fan type 99.5–100 °F, about 57% humidity, eggs turned 5–6 times a day; still-air about 102 °F at egg height. Turn at least twice a day from day 2 to day 18 (FB 1363, p. 3). Homemade: a foam cooler (photos) with a 40-watt bulb and thermostat at 99–100 °F, or a double cardboard box with the bulb in a chimney touching nothing (fire risk) — plan on hatching no more than half the eggs.

Brooding:

  • With the hen: shut hen and chicks in a brood coop (drawing) at night (rats, cats). Move the coop weekly to fresh short grass (FB 1538, p. 18). Raise chicks on ground old birds haven't used for a year (p. 17).
  • Without a hen: start at about 95 °F. Chicks crowding to the heat are cold; panting with open mouths are too hot (p. 22; what each looks like). Wire across corners stops piling and smothering.

📚 Go deeper: USDA FB 1363 — setting hens and choosing eggs; USDA FB 1538, p. 11 — incubation and brooding; Wikipedia: egg incubation — pictures of embryo development.

🥚 Eggs: how many, which hens, storing

On little bought feed, expect far fewer eggs (FAO, p. 9):

How the hens live Eggs per hen per year
Scavenging only 20–30
Scavenging + shelter and disease control 40–60
Some feed about 100
Fully fed 160–220

So two eggs a day (730 a year) takes 7–8 part-fed hens, or 25+ scavengers (our arithmetic).

Which hens to keep (USDA FB 1727): hens lay most in year one and about 20% less in year two. Eat early molters and non-layers in June–July; keep hens still laying in August–September for a second year and for breeding (p. 5). A non-layer has a shrunken, dull, dry comb (p. 8), pubic bones close together, only two fingers from the keel (p. 9; layer vs. non-layer), and yellow returning to beak and shanks (p. 10).

Storing eggs:

  • Collect at least once a day. Wash only dirty eggs, in water 20 °F warmer than the egg (OSU EM 9350, p. 5).
  • Clean, unwashed eggs keep 2–3 weeks at cool room temperature, longer in a cold cellar (rule of thumb).
  • Spring surplus: water glass or limewater crocks keep eggs 6–9 months (USDA 1935, p. 3; FB 1508, p. 32).

📚 Go deeper: USDA FB 1727 — telling layers from loafers, with pictures; USDA Home Methods of Preserving Eggs — water glass and lime; Root Cellar guide — storing eggs cool.

🌡️ Heat, smoke and fire season

Rabbits (USDA FB 1730, p. 31):

  • Newborn litters and does late in pregnancy suffer most.
  • Signs: fast breathing, wet mouth, slight bleeding from the nostrils.
  • Do: shade and airflow (a cooling basket for young rabbits). Wetting hutch tops and floors cools the hutch 6–10 °F. Lay a sick rabbit on a feed sack soaked in cold water.

Chickens (FAO, p. 27): they have no sweat glands and cool by panting. Severe stress above 104 °F (40 °C); 115 °F (46 °C) kills most. They eat less, drink more and lay smaller eggs. Give shade, airflow and cool water; white paint reflects up to 70% of the sun's heat on a roof. Heat-stressed birds peck each other.

Wildfire smoke (PNW 783, p. 10): watch for coughing, open-mouth breathing, watery eyes, weakness. Keep water clean and fresh, cool them, mist down dust, and put off stressful jobs (weaning, moving). Evacuation plan: Illinois Valley page.

📚 Go deeper: USDA FB 1730, p. 31 — rabbits in hot weather; FAO, p. 27 — heat and housing; PNW 783, p. 8 — evacuating or sheltering stock.

🩺 Common diseases with no vet

Rabbits:

  • Quarantine new rabbits a month, downwind, and do their chores last (OSU EC 1654, p. 1).
  • Watch for: runny nose and matted front paws (snuffles; photo), crusty ears (ear mites), diarrhea, sores.
  • The common killers — coccidiosis, pneumonia and enteritis — come mostly from poor sanitation (R-41, p. 52). Keep floors dry, clear droppings, keep droppings out of feed and water (p. 57).
  • Bloat or diarrhea with no fever: more hay and roughage; beware fermented or spoiled feed (p. 57).
  • Snuffles: cull (p. 56) — it spreads.
  • At butchering: white spots on an enlarged liver = liver coccidiosis (p. 56). Throw that liver away.

Chickens:

  • Coccidiosis: bloody droppings, droopy young birds; at death the two blind guts (ceca) are swollen with blood (FB 1652, pp. 52–53; life cycle). If you stocked amprolium, it treats it; chicks kept off their droppings (wire or slat floors, fresh ground) get less (Where There Is No Animal Doctor, p. 317).
  • Marek's disease (paralysis; swollen nerves) strikes at 12–25 weeks (UF PS044, p. 7). No cure — cull, and hatch from survivors' lines (rule of thumb).
  • Lice and mites: ruffled feathers, scabs, fewer eggs (Practical Poultry Raising, p. 132; louse, mite). Give a dust bath of fine dry soil and wood ash, keep nests clean with fresh straw (rule of thumb).
  • Burn or deeply bury birds that die of disease (FB 1652, p. 13).
  • Gloves and eye protection with lye or lime disinfectants (FB 1538, p. 17).

📚 Go deeper: Where There Is No Animal Doctor, p. 312 — poultry diseases with no vet; Raising Healthy Rabbits and Raising Healthy Poultry — illustrated care; USDA FB 1652 — disease signs (ignore its drugs).

🔪 Humane slaughter and butchering

Killing — practice on a carcass first; the AVMA says neck dislocation is humane only when done by trained people on small animals (AVMA, p. 44):

  • Rabbit: hold the hind legs, thumb just behind the ears, fingers under the chin; stretch and raise the head sharply (how to hold it). Or a heavy blow at the base of the skull (FB 1730, p. 52).
  • Chicken: hang it head-down in a killing cone and cut the side of the neck (Kansas State EP71, p. 1).

Chickens (EP71, p. 1; UMN EB 385):

  1. No feed for 8–10 hours before.
  2. Scald at 140 °F for 30–40 seconds, dunking up and down; a little detergent helps. Pluck at once.
  3. Gut (step by step), then chill in ice water to 40 °F (UMN, p. 6); keep unfrozen under 4 days (EP71, p. 2).
  • Don't eat a bird with lumps or spots on the liver, fluid in the body, or orange fat on a thin carcass.
  • Pinfeathery birds: wait a week or two (UMN, p. 1). Allow 1–2 gallons of water per bird (OSU EM 9273, p. 2).

Rabbits (FB 1730, pp. 52–55):

  1. No feed the day before (R-41, p. 77).
  2. Hang by a hook through the hock tendon of one hind leg; cut off the head at once to bleed.
  3. Remove tail, free hind foot and front feet, then peel the skin off like a sock (steps).
  4. Gut. Heart, kidneys and liver are good food. Stretch the pelt on a wire frame to dry (pelt on a stretcher); tan with salt-alum (FB 1334, p. 27).
  • Yield: a 4–4½ lb fryer at 8 weeks dresses to 50–59% of live weight, 78–80% of that edible (p. 47).

Keeping the meat: pressure-can it (soak dressed rabbit 1 hour in salted water first) (USDA canning guide 5, p. 5), or dry thin strips (CVM, p. 90) — 16 oz raw makes about 4 oz jerky (PNW 632, p. 7).

🌱 Manure for the garden

  • Rabbit manure has about 2% nitrogen — more than most farm manures — and won't burn plants: dig it straight in (USDA Rabbit Manure, pp. 4–5). A doe and her litters make about 6 cubic feet a year (9 with bedding); a buck about 3. Raise earthworms in the pile under the hutches (vermicomposting) — they cut odor and flies (p. 6).
  • Chicken manure: compost it at least 120 days (compost bins) before use, and never put it raw on seedlings or growing plants. Spread raw litter in fall, after harvest, so winter rains leach out the salts (Idaho CIS 1194, pp. 2, 6) — our wet winters suit this.

📚 Go deeper: USDA Value and Use of Rabbit Manure, p. 6 — composting and worm beds; Idaho CIS 1194 — the 120-day rule and spreading rates.

🧬 Keeping the lines going for years

How many feed a family (our arithmetic from the sources):

  • A doe at full production raises about 28 fryers a year — roughly 60 lb of carcass, 48 lb of meat (FB 1730, pp. 19, 47). Each pound of fryer takes 3½–4½ lb of feed. Rabbit twice a week for four people ≈ 100 fryers ≈ 4 does + 1 buck; keep 6 does and 2 bucks to cover losses and smaller litters on home feed.
  • FAO's village flock: 15 hens laying 30 eggs each = 450 eggs, some set under broodies (FAO, p. 36). About half of each hatch is cockerels — eat them at 12–20 weeks before they compete for feed (rule of thumb).

Avoid inbreeding:

  • Inbreeding isn't harmful in itself, but it quickly brings out hidden defects; beginners usually inbreed just to avoid getting a new buck (FB 1730, p. 20). Keep at least two unrelated bucks and trade bucks with neighbors every few generations (rule of thumb).
  • Roosters: one per 12–15 Leghorn-type hens, 10–12 Rock or Wyandotte, 10 or fewer of the heaviest; with 20–30 hens keep two and alternate them (FB 1508, p. 10). FAO replaces the cock with a new cockerel every year (p. 36). Trade for an unrelated rooster every 2–3 years, or keep 2–3 hen families and rotate roosters (rule of thumb).
  • Keep records of matings and litter sizes (a hutch card); breed from the best mothers.

Don't live on rabbit. It is high in protein but low in fat (EC 1651, p. 1). People who live mainly on lean meat get "rabbit starvation" within 1–2 weeks — nausea, diarrhea, weakness and a craving for fat that more lean meat won't fix. Keep protein under about a third of calories and pair rabbit with fat (egg yolks, chicken fat, lard, nuts) and starch (rule of thumb).

⚠️ Old advice in the library to ignore

  • Sodium fluoride dusted on setting hens for lice — FB 1363, p. 9. It is poisonous. Instead: a dust bath of dry soil and wood ash, clean straw in the nest.
  • Poisons as wormers and mite killers in FB 1652: carbon tetrachloride (p. 63, p. 77), nicotine sulfate (p. 78), DDT (p. 49), phenothiazine (p. 79), kerosene emulsion and carbolineum (p. 7). Instead: use the bulletin for signs and prevention only; rotate ground, keep litter dry, cull. Also nicotine sulfate painted on roosts in Practical Poultry Raising, p. 132 — don't.
  • Lindane for rabbit ear mites — Peace Corps Livestock Manual Vol 4, p. 341. Banned and nerve-toxic.
  • "Salt-acid" tanning with concentrated sulfuric acid — FB 1730, p. 60. Instead: salt-alum (p. 61; FB 1334, p. 27).
  • Formalin for tanning and preserving paws in R-41, pp. 81, 83 — toxic; use salt-alum. Ipil-ipil (leucaena) as feed (p. 37) — toxic in quantity (not grown here anyway). Many drugs R-41 names won't be available.
  • Cresol disinfectant — FB 1508, p. 31. Toxic. Instead: scrub, sun and dry; lime with gloves and goggles.
  • "Don't mix your own rabbit feed" (OSU EC 1655, p. 1) is right while pellets exist. After that, home-grown feed is the only choice — expect slower growth and smaller litters, not failure.
