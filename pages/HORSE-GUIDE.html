🐴 Horses, Mules and Draft Animals

How to keep, feed, handle, ride, shoe and pack horses, mules and donkeys, and how to put them to work in harness — written for a time when there may be no feed store, vet or farrier. The manuals are in Horses-Draft-Animals\ (Care-Feeding, Riding-Training, Hoof-Care-Shoeing, Packing-Travel, Draft-Harness). Horse health, vital signs, colic and first aid are in Animals\ and the Animals and vet care section of the Illinois Valley page.

⚠️ Rules that prevent the worst accidents

  • A horse can kill you without meaning to. Stay where it can see you, speak before you touch it, keep a hand on it as you move around the hindquarters, and never stand directly behind or in front of it.
  • Never wrap a lead rope or reins around your hand or body, and never tie a horse with a knot you can't pull loose. Never tie with a chain lead.
  • Wear a helmet and boots with a heel when riding.
  • The old books here (1867–1940s) teach good handling and shoeing but also harsh "breaking" methods and dangerous old medicines (purges, firing, chloroform). Use them for skills, not for doctoring.

🐎 Choosing an animal

  • For a first horse, pick an older, calm, well-trained one (roughly 8–15 years) over a young or green horse. Check it sound before you take it: walk and trot it on hard ground, look for limping, swellings, bad feet and bad habits. (USDA How to Select a Sound Horse)
  • Age by the teeth: after about 12 years the front teeth turn triangular and slant forward. (USDA AIB 353)
  • Mules are tough, sure-footed, eat less and handle heat and rough country better; they tend to stand and fight rather than bolt. A well-trained mule is very steady; a badly trained one is hard work. Donkeys need shelter always — their coats aren't waterproof, which matters in our wet winters. (Donkey Care Handbook)

🌾 Feeding and water

  • Total feed: 1.5–2.5% of body weight a day, most of it hay or pasture. Never less than 8–10 lb of hay a day for a 1,000-lb horse; an idle horse holds weight on about 18 lb of good hay. (Nebraska G1781; FM 25-5)
  • Weigh a horse with a string: weight (lb) = heart girth (in) × heart girth (in) × body length (in) ÷ 330. Body length is point of shoulder to point of buttock. (G1781)
  • Working horses: about 1.1 lb grain + 1.25 lb hay per 100 lb of body weight a day, grain split into 3 feeds. Keep each grain meal under about 5 lb for a 1,000-lb horse. Feed by weight: a quart of oats ≈ 1 lb, a quart of corn ≈ 1.7 lb. (USDA FB1419; UGA B1356)
  • Water: 5–15 gallons a day, double in heat or hard work. Give an exhausted horse small drinks every 5–10 minutes until it recovers. In cold weather, take the chill off the water or salt the feed so it keeps drinking (prevents impaction colic). (UGA B1356; FM 25-5)
  • Salt: about 3 oz a day; loose salt-mineral beats a block. Western Oregon soils are low in selenium — use a mineral that contains it. (Rule of thumb) (AIB 353)
  • Army feeding rules that prevent colic: water before feeding (or wait an hour after); never grain a hot horse (hay is fine); a little hay before grain; change feeds gradually over 1–2 weeks; never feed moldy or dusty hay. (FM 25-5)
  • Body condition: keep them at 5–7 on the 1–9 scale — feel ribs, back and tailhead with your hands; a winter coat hides thinness. (G1781)

🦷 Teeth and worms

  • Teeth: check at least yearly. Sharp edges form on the outside of the upper molars and inside of the lowers — float (rasp) them or the horse drops feed and loses weight. (PNW 587; Merillat Animal Dentistry)
  • Worms: don't deworm blindly every 8 weeks. Adults need 1–2 treatments a year (one in fall for bots and tapeworms); youngsters 3–4. When dewormers run out: pick up manure, don't overgraze, rotate pastures and graze with cattle or sheep. (Penn State/SARE)

🦶 Hoof care and shoeing

  • Pick out the feet every day. A healthy frog feels like firm rubber. Thrush (black, stinking rot in the frog) comes from wet, dirty footing — trim away dead tissue, apply an antiseptic, and get the horse onto dry ground. In our wet winters, give horses a dry gravel or wood-chip standing area. (TM 2-220; PNW 587; OSU EC1558)
  • Hooves grow about ⅜ inch a month. Trim barefoot horses every 6–12 weeks, donkeys and mules every 6–10; the Army reset shoes every 3–6 weeks. (NMSU B-707; TM 2-220)
  • Going barefoot (when shoes and nails run out): leave the sole, balance the wall, round off the outer edge so it doesn't chip, and check weekly. Most horses do fine barefoot on soft ground; hard rocky work wears feet faster than they grow. (TM 2-220)
  • Making and fitting shoes: TM 2-220 The Horseshoer teaches forging shoes from bar stock, fitting and nailing, and even shoeing in the field with a ground fire and a rail for an anvil. Lungwitz's Text-Book of Horseshoeing is the standard reference. Pair with the Blacksmithing guide.

🤠 Handling, saddling and riding

  • Leading: from the left side, right hand near the halter, slack folded (never wrapped) in your left hand. (PNW 587)
  • Tying: use a quick-release knot, about 3 ft of rope, tied at or above withers height to something solid. Any rope around the neck gets a bowline (it can't tighten), never a slip knot. Untie before taking the halter off. (PNW 587)
  • Saddle fit: with pad and rider on, the gullet must clear the withers. After a sweaty ride, even sweat marks = good fit; dry spots = pressure. A sliding saddle needs a breast collar, not a tighter cinch. (UF Saddle Fit)
  • Cinching: snug it gradually until a flat hand just slips under, walk the horse a few steps, then recheck after a short ride — many horses puff up their bellies. On the trail, stop after 1–2 miles to retighten. (PNW 587; Animal Management)
  • Mounting and riding: mount from the left, reins short enough to stop the horse, sit deep with heels down. The 4-H manual (PNW 587) and FM 25-5 walk through riding step by step.
  • Starting young horses: handle foals early and teach them to lead before weaning. Light harness or saddle work at 2–3 years; no heavy work until they're nearly mature. First tie with a neck rope through the halter ring, tied with a bowline. (USDA FB1368)
  • Tack repair: cutting leather, hand-stitching with waxed thread and riveting are in TM 10-430 The Saddler and Hasluck's Harness Making.

🎒 Travel and packing

Walk / trot about 4 mph / 8–9 mph
Steady day's travel 20+ miles on easy ground, 10–15 in mountains
Rider no more than about 20% of the horse's weight (250 lb max)
Pack load 200–250 lb per 900–1,000-lb animal; 300 lb only short-distance
Load balance side packs within 2–3 lb of each other

(FM 25-5; FM 25-7; USFS Instructions on Horse Packing; Montana outfitters manual)

  • On the road: rest 5–10 minutes every hour; every 2–3 hours unsaddle, let the horse roll and graze. Walk them up steep hills. Only hardened horses can do 40–50 miles a day for days on end. Condition animals 2–4 weeks before hard travel. (Animal Management)
  • Packing: sawbuck and Decker pack saddles, hitches and loading are in the 1943 Forest Service manual (written for Oregon and Washington) and FM 25-7, which also shows improvised pack saddles and a travois.

🚜 Harness and draft work

  • What an animal can pull all day: about one-tenth of its body weight in steady draft (a big horse pulling 150 lb at 2½ mph ≈ 1 horsepower). Donkeys pull about a fifth of their weight for 3–4 hours. A cart donkey should pull no more than twice its weight, cart included, on the flat. (Peace Corps Animal Traction; Donkey Care Handbook)
  • Collar fit: a flat hand should just pass between the collar and the windpipe. A short collar chokes; a long one bruises the shoulders. Give each horse its own collar and wipe it after every use. Sore shoulders are the #1 harness problem. (USDA FB1419; FM 25-5)
  • Harness, hitching, driving pairs and teams, carts, plows and sledges: FM 25-5, Mounted Instruction for Field Artillery, the Peace Corps Animal Traction manual and Starkey's Harnessing and Implements. Big-team hitches and eveners: Sommerfeld's How to Hitch Horses.

☠️ Local dangers

  • Toxic plants: tansy ragwort, poison and water hemlock, foxglove and bracken fern — hungry horses on overgrazed pasture eat them. (Wild-Plants\ and Animals\ sheets)
  • Wildfire: evacuate horses early and in daylight; practice loading into a trailer ahead of time. See the Illinois Valley page.

Old military feeding rules assume plenty of oats and hay; scale rations to what you can grow, and watch body condition.
