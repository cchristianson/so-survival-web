🚰 Water Purification — With What You Have

How to make creek, spring, rain or well water safe to drink when there's no store and no power: settling and straining, homemade sand, biosand and clay-pot filters, charcoal (what it does and doesn't do), boiling, bleach and pool shock, sunlight, solar stills, simple tests, and safe storage. The manuals are in Water-Purification\ (Filters-Sand-Charcoal-Ceramic, Disinfection, Settling-Coagulation, Overviews-Testing), Water\ and Homestead-Skills\Sanitation-Water. Getting the water out of the ground is in the Wells & Pumps guide.

The four rules of safe water

  1. Clear it, then kill the germs. Settle or filter muddy water first; then boil or disinfect.
  2. Charcoal and sand filters alone do NOT make water safe. They clear water and improve taste; charcoal beds even grow bacteria and shield them from chlorine. Charcoal goes last, after disinfection (WHO, pp. 51–52; Wilderness Medical Society, p. 6).
  3. Boiling kills germs but removes no chemicals — not fuel, metals, salt or fire-retardant runoff.
  4. Keep treated water clean: covered container with a spout, never dip a cup into it.

🧭 Which method does what

From CAWST's comparison of household methods (CAWST Fact Sheets):

Method Germs (bacteria / viruses / Giardia-Crypto) Muddy water Chemicals Notes
Settling, three-pot storage some very good no First step for muddy water
Cloth straining larger organisms only some no Quick first step
Alum, wood ash, Moringa seed some (lab good, field ~50%) very good no Clears water fast
Biosand / slow sand filter good / fair / very good very good no (some iron) Needs a living top layer
Ceramic pot filter very good / fair / very good very good no Needs silver coating for best results
Boiling kills all no no Uses fuel
Chlorine (bleach, pool shock) kills bacteria & viruses; weak on Crypto no no Cheap, protects stored water
Sunlight (SODIS) good needs clear water no Free; sunny months only
Solar still / distilling kills all yes removes salts & metals Slow, ~1 gallon/day per 12 sq ft
Charcoal / "activated" carbon poor — not a germ barrier some taste, odor, many organic chemicals Use last

WHO's bar for a "protective" method is removing about 99% of bacteria and protozoa and 99.9% of viruses; most homemade methods reach that only in combination (WHO Evaluating Household Water Treatment, p. 11). Best simple combination: settle or filter → boil or chlorinate → (optional) charcoal for taste.

🫙 Step 1 — Clear the water

  • Let it settle. Three-pot method: fill pot 1 from the source; each day pour pot 1 gently into pot 2, and pot 2 into pot 3 (your drinking pot) — water sits at least 2 days and much of the dirt and some germs settle out. Still disinfect it (CAWST, p. 4).
  • Strain through cloth: old, soft cotton folded 7–8 times catches particles bigger than about 20 microns — silt, worms, the plankton cholera rides on — but not bacteria or viruses. Wash the cloth after each use (CAWST, pp. 14–15).
  • Make the mud drop (coagulants):
    • Alum (pickling alum): about ⅛ tsp per gallon; stir fast 1 minute, slowly 5 minutes, settle at least 30 minutes, pour off through cloth (WMS, p. 7).
    • Wood ash, lime, crushed beans also work as rough coagulants (same source).
    • Moringa seed: about 1 crushed kernel per liter of muddy water — shake the paste in a cup of water, strain it in, stir 1 minute fast and 5–10 minutes slow, settle 1–2 hours (ECHO note; WEDC, p. 3). Moringa won't grow here (frost) — stockpile seed if you want it.
    • Coagulants clear water; they don't make it safe. Always disinfect afterward.
  • Is it clear enough? Look down through a clear 2-liter bottle of the water at large print — if you can read it, it's under about 50 NTU, clear enough for filters, SODIS and chlorine (CAWST Testing, p. 81).

🏺 Step 2 — Homemade filters

Biosand filter (best household filter you can build)

A concrete or plastic box of sand with a living layer on top (the "schmutzdecke") that eats germs (CAWST Biosand Construction Manual):

  • Layers, bottom to top: 2 in of drainage gravel (¼–½ in), 2 in of small gravel, then fine washed sand (0.7 mm or finer) filled with water already in the box so no air gets trapped (pp. 59, 92).
  • About 2 in of water must stand above the sand when it stops flowing — more starves the living layer of air; letting it dry out kills it. A diffuser plate keeps poured water from digging up the top (pp. 18–19).
  • Use it every day, one bucket (12–18 L) at a time, 6–12 hours apart. The living layer takes up to 30 days to grow — disinfect the output until then and always for babies and the sick. When flow gets slow, stir the top inch and scoop off the dirty water ("swirl and dump") (CAWST Fact Sheets, pp. 18–19).
  • Results once mature: bacteria about 88–98% removed in the field, Giardia-type parasites over 99.9%, viruses less reliable (p. 19).
  • Cold winter water slows the living layer — filter slower and disinfect (EPA slow sand, p. 9).

Slow sand filter (for a group or homestead)

The same idea, bigger: water trickles down through about 3 ft of fine sand at 0.1–0.2 m per hour; build two beds so one can be cleaned (scrape off the top inch). A mature filter removes 95–100% of fecal bacteria and nearly all viruses and cysts. Takes about 3 weeks to "ripen" when new (IRC Slow Sand Filtration, pp. 38, 48).

The bucket "layer filter" — gravel, sand, charcoal

A bucket of gravel, sand and charcoal clears and improves water but is not a germ barrier. The right way to use charcoal is the three-stage system: gravel roughing filter → slow sand biofilter (germs) → crushed charcoal (chemicals and taste) → covered storage; replace the charcoal yearly (Aqueous Solutions Biochar Water Filter, pp. 2–9).

Clay pot (ceramic) filter

Clay mixed with sawdust or rice husk (about 50:50 by volume to start) is pressed into a pot and fired to about 900 °C (1,650 °F) — the sawdust burns out and leaves tiny pores (CMWG Ceramic Pot Filters, p. 62; RDIC handbook). It sits in a covered bucket with a tap and gives 1–3 liters an hour. Test local clay first (clay testing); flow-test each pot (at least 1 liter in the first hour, not too fast — too fast means cracks) (CMWG, p. 114). They work best with a colloidal silver coating, which you can't make from raw materials — without it, still disinfect. Replace every 1–2 years or when cracked. Kiln plans: Mani kiln.

⚫ Charcoal: making it and using it right

  • What it does: soaks up taste, odor and many organic chemicals (pesticides, some fuel residues). What it doesn't: reliably remove germs — WHO doesn't recommend it for germs, and bacteria grow in the bed (WHO, pp. 51–52).
  • Plain vs. activated: ordinary charcoal has its pores clogged with tar, so it adsorbs poorly. Factories "activate" it by heating to about 800 °C in steam, which opens the pores (FAO Industrial Charcoal Making, p. 28).
  • Homemade water-treatment charcoal (Aqueous Solutions Char Drum Oven):
    1. Pack dry wood pieces (1–2 in thick) tightly in a steel drum, set a chimney drum on top, and light it from the top. It burns about 2 hours down to faint blue flames.
    2. Quench with plenty of water — the steam partly opens the pores (crude activation, not true activated carbon).
    3. Crush and sieve to 1–5 mm pieces; wash out the dust.
  • Use it as the LAST step after the water is filtered and disinfected, or in a column after a sand filter; replace it yearly. Hardwood (oak, madrone) makes good charcoal. Chemical activation (acids, zinc chloride) isn't a safe home method (rule of thumb).
  • Charcoal-making in bulk: Practical Action brief and the Peace Corps charcoal manual.

📚 Go deeper: FAO — what activated charcoal is, p. 28; Aqueous Solutions — charcoal oven; Wikipedia: activated carbon.

🔥 Step 3 — Kill the germs

Boiling — the surest method. A rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft — we're far below that) kills bacteria, viruses and parasites even in cloudy water. Let it cool covered; don't add ice (WHO Boil Water, p. 1; OSU, p. 5). Fuel cost: about 0.2 kg (½ lb) of wood per liter — a family's 4 gallons a day is about 7 lb of wood a day (WHO Bulletin, p. 4) — see the Firewood guide.

Bleach (plain unscented, 6–8.25% sodium hypochlorite) — EPA, p. 1:

Water 6% bleach 8.25% bleach
1 quart, clear 2 drops 2 drops
1 gallon, clear 8 drops 6 drops
1 gallon, cloudy or very cold 16 drops 12 drops

Stir, wait 30 minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine; if not, dose again and wait 15 more minutes. Cold water needs longer: 1 hour at 50–65 °F, 2+ hours below 50 °F — winter creek water (CAWST, p. 59). Bleach loses strength — use it within a year. Chlorine is weak against Cryptosporidium: settle or filter first.

Pool shock (calcium hypochlorite) — stores for years as dry granules. Dissolve 1 heaping teaspoon in 2 gallons of water to make a stock solution; then use 1 part stock to 100 parts water (1 pint per 12½ gallons), wait 30 minutes (EPA, p. 2). Use plain granules (no algaecide or stabilizer), mix outdoors with eye protection, and make stock in small batches — it lasts about a month in heat, longer cool and dark (CDC Safe Water Systems, p. 62).

Iodine: 5 drops of 2% tincture per quart (10 if cloudy), wait 30 minutes — fine short-term, not for months on end or for pregnant women (EPA, p. 2).

Sunlight (SODIS): clear plastic (PET) bottles of clear water, lying flat in full sun 6 hours (2 days if more than half cloudy). Works well here March–September, poorly in the winter rains (EAWAG SODIS manual, p. 23).

Solar pasteurizing: water held at 65 °C (150 °F) is pasteurized — a solar box cooker can do several gallons on a summer day (Ciochetti & Metcalf). A lump of beeswax in a sealed tube melts at about 62 °C and shows when it's done (CAWST, p. 76).

📚 Go deeper: EPA Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water; CDC Safe Water Systems handbook — making and storing chlorine; Wilderness Medical Society guidelines; Wikipedia: SODIS.

☀️ Salty or chemical water: the solar still

A shallow black basin under sloped glass or clear plastic: the sun evaporates the water, it condenses on the cover and runs into a gutter — leaving salt, metals and germs behind. Expect about 1 gallon a day per 10–12 sq ft of glass (CAWST, p. 71; Aprovecho, p. 43). Some fuel-type chemicals can carry over (rule of thumb).

🔬 Simple tests

  • Turbidity tube: a clear tube with a black-and-white disk at the bottom — pour water in until the disk disappears; the depth tells cloudiness (disk gone at 18 cm ≈ 50 NTU; at 85 cm ≈ 5 NTU) (Myre & Shaw, pp. 11–16).
  • H₂S bacteria test: a paper strip with a special medium in a bottle of water turns black in 1–2 days if fecal bacteria are present — keep it warm (above 77 °F). Agreed with lab tests about 90% of the time; sulfur well water can give false alarms (WHO, pp. 16, 33). The test bottles must be bought ahead.
  • Chlorine check: a faint chlorine smell 30 minutes after dosing means enough was added.
  • Sanitary check: look at the source — animals, outhouses, runoff upstream? Fix the source first (CAWST Testing).

📚 Go deeper: CAWST Introduction to Drinking Water Quality Testing; Turbidity tube.

🪣 Storing water safely

  • Covered container with a tap or narrow spout, opaque, easy to clean; never put untreated water in it; clean it with soap or chlorine; keep it off the ground in shade; pour, don't dip (CAWST, pp. 83–84).
  • Don't reuse milk jugs — they can't be cleaned well (OSU, p. 2).
  • Chlorinated water keeps best; boiled water can be recontaminated — drink it within a day or two.

🔥 After a wildfire

Burned slopes send ash, metals, nitrate and organic chemicals into creeks after storms — levels jumped 10 to 10,000 times after a Colorado fire (USGS, p. 1). Boiling, chlorine, sunlight and sand or clay filters do not remove these. Fire-damaged plastic pipes can leach benzene, and boiling doesn't remove it (WQA). After a fire, prefer springs or wells outside the burn, or rainwater; distilling and charcoal are the only partial chemical barriers here.
