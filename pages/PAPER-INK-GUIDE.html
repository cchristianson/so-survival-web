📜 Paper, Ink & Toilet Paper

How to make the paper, ink and toilet paper a household goes through once the stores are gone: paper by hand from rags, plants and scrap paper; iron-gall and soot inks; pens, pencils and slates; keeping paper and books from mold; and what to use instead of toilet paper, tissues and paper towels, and where each may go. The two papermaking and ink books are in Homestead-Skills\Paper-Ink: Watt's The Art of Paper-Making (1890), whose chapter "Making Paper by Hand" covers the vat, mould and sizing, and the Bureau of Standards' Inks (Circular 426, 1940). The old recipe books are in Homestead-Skills\Household-Make-Do and the toilet books in Sanitation-Hygiene\Latrines-Toilets. Where no book covers a step, this guide says (rule of thumb).

The short version

  • Rags make the best paper. Linen and cotton, cooked soft in weak lye or lime and pounded to pulp; there is "no better material" than linen (Watt, p. 20).
  • Lye burns skin and can blind. Gloves, goggles, no aluminum pots, children away; flush splashes with lots of water (Practical Action Soapmaking, p. 1).
  • Scrap paper is the easy win: soak, pound, form again (Leland, p. 159).
  • Iron-gall ink is tannin + iron + a little gum: oak galls, tanoak bark or sumac leaves, with rusty nails in vinegar (Practical Action Natural Dyeing, p. 4). Keep tannin and iron about equal by weight; too much iron eats paper (NBS Inks, p. 35).
  • Soot ink never fades and doesn't harm paper (NBS, p. 37): use it for records that must last.
  • Never add mercury ("corrosive sublimate") or chromates as old recipes do. A few cloves or a little alcohol stop mold (Household Discoveries, p. 422).
  • Washing with water is the cleanest toilet paper, 2 cups to 3 quarts a time (Compendium, p. 10). Cloth wipes belong to one person and are washed hot. Only toilet paper goes into a septic or flush toilet (EPA, p. 6).
  • Learn poison oak and stinging nettle before you wipe with any leaf. Wipe front to back and wash your hands (Where There Is No Doctor, ch. 18, p. 10).

🧺 What paper is, and fibers that work

Paper is "a composition of linen, cotton, or other vegetable fibre reduced to powder and then combined with size, which is a kind of glue" (Leland, p. 112). Beaten fiber spread thin on a screen felts into a sheet; the size (glue) lets it take ink.

Rags: remove buttons, hooks, rubber and pins; open seams, hems and knots; cut 2- to 4-inch squares; keep dark rags apart from white (Watt, pp. 36, 38). Old rags spread disease (p. 27), so boil a sick person's rags before handling them (rule of thumb). Stalks: ret, break and scutch them like flax ("Linen, nettle and native fibers" in the Clothing & Leather guide).

🔥 Cooking fiber soft

Boiling in alkali removes grease, dirt and the "cementing substances" around the fibers; linen rags lose a fifth to a third of their weight (Watt, p. 45). ⚠️ Lye "can cause blindness if splashed into the eye" (Practical Action, p. 1): gloves, long sleeves, goggles, children away, no aluminum (CAWST, p. 2); flush splashes with plenty of water. Wet lime is caustic too (rule of thumb).

  1. Liquor: wood-ash lye ("Soap from fat and ashes" in the Make-Do guide); potash "affects but slightly" the fiber itself (Watt, p. 19). Or slaked lime, gentler on fine rags (p. 50), which helps pulp "felt together better" (Lime circular, p. 25).
  2. How much: mills used about 5 lb of lime and 3 lb of soda ash per 100 lb of rags (Watt, p. 49), under 1 oz of lime and ½ oz of washing soda per pound (arithmetic). For ash lye, thin egg-floating lye with about 4 parts water (rule of thumb).
  3. Simmer until a fiber pulls apart easily in your fingers, the old test for "tender" rags (Watt, p. 147). Mills boiled 2–6 hours under steam pressure (p. 49); an open pot takes half a day or more (rule of thumb).
  4. Rinse until the water runs clear and the fiber stops feeling slippery, "until free of soda" (Lime circular 337, p. 92). Gloves on until then.
  5. No fuel? Old mills retted rags, piling them damp until tender, with "considerable loss of fibre" to rot (Watt, p. 147).

🔨 Beating fiber into pulp

  • It is slow. Stamping rods took "forty pairs of stamps for twenty-four hours to produce one hundredweight of paper" (Watt, pp. 147–148). Pound wet fiber with a wooden mallet on a hardwood block, or in a mortar as Leland does (p. 159); plan on an hour or more for a few dozen sheets (rule of thumb).
  • Don't chop fibers short: cut fiber makes paper that "will be tender" (weak). Long pulp makes strong paper, shorter pulp fine writing paper; grasses beat in half the time of rags (Watt, p. 129).
  • Ready when a pinch shaken in a jar of water makes an even cloud (rule of thumb): "a fine paste, not … lumpy and stringy, but soft" (Leland, p. 113).

🪟 The mould, deckle and vat

In Watt (pp. 147–149) the mould is a wooden frame "neatly joined at the corners", with ribs a little over an inch apart and fine wires along it, "about fifteen or twenty in an inch". The deckle, a loose frame on top, "must fit very neatly or the edges of the paper will be rough". The vat was a warmed tub "about 5 feet square and 4 feet deep"; felts of wool cloth go between wet sheets.

Build a small one (rule of thumb, after Watt):

  1. Make two identical frames of straight, dry 1×2 wood, a bit bigger inside than your sheet (about 9 × 12 in for letter paper), corners glued and pegged square.
  2. On one (the mould), fit thin ribs about 1 inch apart, flush with the top, and tack fine window screen tight over them (fiberglass, brass or bronze; steel rusts and stains).
  3. The other frame is the deckle; it must sit snugly on the screen. Oil or wax all the wood.
  4. Vat: a washtub or tote longer than the mould. Felts: two dozen pieces of wool blanket or flannel.

📄 Forming, couching, pressing and drying

  1. Charge the vat with clean water and pulp, and stir before every sheet to keep it "uniformly mixed" (Watt, p. 148). More pulp, thicker sheets (rule of thumb).
  2. Dip the mould with its deckle and lift "as much of the pulp as will form a sheet of paper" (same page).
  3. Hold it level as it drains, "otherwise … the sheet of paper would be thicker on one part than another". Shake gently front to back, then side to side, "propelling the pulp in four directions" (p. 149). Lift off the deckle.
  4. Couch (say "cooch"): turn the mould face down onto a damp felt, press, and lift it away. Lay another felt on top; keep stacking felt, sheet, felt (same page).
  5. Press hard: the mill used "a very powerful press" (same page); at home, boards above and below and a car jack under a beam, a cider press or heavy stones (rule of thumb). Then take the sheets off the felts, press them gently to remove felt marks, and next day separate and press once more (same page).
  6. Dry slowly, hung over lines; cow-hair ropes "do not stain the paper" (p. 150), and slow loft drying was thought best (p. 145). In wet winters, dry indoors away from direct stove heat (rule of thumb).
  7. Smooth: mills "hot-pressed" sheets between pasteboards with hot iron plates (p. 150); a warm flatiron over a cloth does the same (rule of thumb).

🧴 Sizing: making paper take ink

On unsized paper a pen stroke "would spread in all directions" (Watt, p. 136). Starch stirred into the pulp won't fix it: blotting paper is made "with starch alone" (p. 199). Hand-made paper was dipped in gelatine size after drying (p. 150).

  • Size from hide scraps: cover clean skin clippings with water and hold at 180–190 °F, "very important that the liquid should not actually boil", for 12–16 hours (pp. 140–141). Skim, strain, and stir in dissolved alum, which "greatly improves its sizing property, besides preserving it" (p. 141); more in warm weather (p. 143). Hide scraps and alum: Clothing & Leather guide.
  • Quicker: boil "clean parchment cuttings until they form a clear solution" and strain through muslin (Henley's, p. 508), or use "1 ounce of good glue in 1 pint of hot water" (Household Discoveries, p. 271).
  • Sizing: warm the size to lukewarm (Watt, p. 143); lay dry sheets in one at a time, each evenly wet; press out the extra, separate, press again, dry slowly (p. 150).
  • Thin paper needs stronger size. Sizing is "one of the most difficult and uncertain" jobs, upset by heat and damp (p. 143): test a few sheets first.
  • Starch brushed on the surface helps: bond paper was surface-sized with starch, "an additional barrier against the penetration of the ink" (NBS Inks, p. 39). Brush thin cooked starch onto dry sheets (rule of thumb).
  • Size and starch feed silverfish (Leland, p. 144); a few drops of clove or other essential oil keep paste from molding (Household Discoveries, p. 59).

♻️ Recycling scrap paper, cardboard and mending

Re-pulping: tear scraps small and soak them a long time, changing the water now and then; pound the soft mass in a mortar, boil it, and "to give it consistency, add flour-paste or gum" (Leland, p. 159).

  • Handwritten paper is easier than print: "a little soda-ash will discharge ordinary writing ink", but printer's ink is carbon and leaves grey pulp (Watt, p. 104), fine for wrapping, card and the outhouse. Mills used 10–20 lb of soda ash per 100 lb of paper, boiled 12–24 hours (p. 105). Washing soda: "Laundry" in the Make-Do guide.
  • Cardboard: paste sheets together with size, or lay several wet sheets on each other as you couch (Watt, p. 200). Leland's paste for it has "a little alum and glue and a few drops of oil of cloves" to stop souring (p. 151). Papier-mâché: newspaper strips in flour paste, several layers, dried in the sun (A Book for Midwives, ch. 25, p. 6).
  • Mending: fill holes with pulp worked to a fine paste (Leland, p. 113); patch tears with thin paper and a paste of ½ tsp each starch and flour cooked thick with boiling water, which "will not mold or discolor paper" (Lee's, p. 204).
  • Waxed paper: rub paper on a hot iron plate with beeswax (Henley's, pp. 507–508). Dirty pages: rub with stale bread (Household Discoveries, p. 60).

✒️ Iron-gall ink

Iron and tannin from galls make a pale ink that the air turns black (NBS Inks, p. 7); it "turned black on the paper" (Household Discoveries, p. 416), darkest in about a week (NBS, p. 26).

  • Tannin: oak galls (the old imported kind held 60–70% tannic acid); crush, steep "in hot, not boiling, water", and strain (Household Discoveries, p. 416). Also sumac leaves (Practical Action, p. 4), tanoak bark, "high tannin content" (Tanoak guide, p. 2), and walnut hulls or maple bark (Trapper manual, p. 46). Local tribes blackened basket material with fresh oak bark, water and rusty nails (Chesnut, p. 354).
  • Iron: "rusty nails and a cupful of vinegar" in a bucket of water for "a couple of weeks" (Practical Action, p. 4), or iron filings in ½ gallon of vinegar for several days, some always left undissolved (USDA FB 1334, p. 19).
  • Copperas (green vitriol) is iron sulfate and "contains no copper"; blue vitriol (bluestone) is copper sulfate. Don't mix them up (NBS, p. 36). Good copperas is pale green; rusty means air has spoiled it, so keep it tightly closed (p. 20).

The balance matters. Government record ink has about 31 g of tannin (tannic plus gallic acid) to 30 g of iron sulfate per liter (NBS, p. 13), about 1 oz of each per quart (arithmetic). With too little tannin, writing "may become rusty in a comparatively few years" (p. 26); iron sulfate "caused decided weakening of the paper", tannin did not (p. 35), and some old manuscripts have crumbled to their margins (p. 34). When unsure, use less iron.

With galls and copperas (Household Discoveries, p. 417): 2 oz crushed galls, 1 oz gum arabic, 1 oz copperas, 16 oz soft water; stand 2 weeks, shaking often. That is about 1.3 oz tannin to 1 oz copperas, close to the government balance (arithmetic). Leave out the "5 grains of corrosive sublimate" the book adds: it is mercury. Use a few cloves.

No-store recipe (rule of thumb, from the parts above):

  1. Start the iron water weeks ahead.
  2. Tannin tea: 2 oz crushed galls (or a double handful of chopped tanoak bark or dried sumac leaves) steeped overnight in 1 pint of hot, not boiling, rain water; strain through cloth.
  3. In a glass jar, add iron water a spoonful at a time, testing a stroke on paper after each; stop when the strokes dry dark and stop improving. "Some experiments will be required" (Household Discoveries, p. 417).
  4. Stir in a little gum or thin hide-glue size, which keeps the black from settling and fastens it to the paper (NBS, p. 8). Add a few cloves and bottle it.
  • Rain water (distilled is best); hard water uses up the ink's acid (NBS, p. 18). Glass or a glazed crock: "avoid the use of metal vessels … Iron, especially" (p. 77).
  • It "corrodes steel pens" (Household Discoveries, p. 416): wipe nibs. Don't blot; blotted writing lasts less well (NBS, p. 27).
  • Ink powder keeps: "Powdered nutgalls 4 ounces, copperas 3 ounces, logwood 1 ounce, gum arabic ½ ounce; sufficient for 1 quart of water" (Lee's, p. 61). Travelers pressed such powders into "ink stones" (Henley's, p. 409).

🖤 Soot ink and other inks

Carbon ink is the oldest: lampblack (soot) or charcoal held in glue, gelatin or gum; India-ink cakes are that paste dried, then rubbed with water (NBS Inks, p. 7). Carbon "cannot be bleached by any amount of exposure to intense light" (p. 37) and is unlikely to harm paper (p. 38).

Homemade India ink (Household Discoveries, p. 419): boil an old kid glove (or a clean hide scrap) "until it forms a thick size"; hold a cold plate in a candle flame and, while it is still warm, mix the soot with the size. Test: let lines dry bone dry and sponge them. "If the ink runs, it is of poor quality."

  • Grind it fine and stir before each dip; coarse carbon settles (NBS, pp. 37–38). Never mix it with iron-gall ink: acid makes the carbon settle (p. 38).
  • A tenth part glycerine keeps India ink liquid (Henley's, p. 409); it works better with a brush than a steel pen (p. 408).
  • Printing ink is carbon in boiled linseed or nut oil (Household Discoveries, p. 422). The Wintu and other tribes "used the soot from burnt oak galls to make tattoos" (Tanoak guide, p. 2).
  • Sumac ink: "The berries and leaves of red or staghorn sumac boiled together in water make a permanent black ink" (Seton, p. 334). The Navajo dyed black with three-leaved sumac (Navajo Native Dyes, p. 67), but three leaflets is also poison oak: pick no three-leaflet shrub unless someone who knows both shows you (rule of thumb).
  • Berry juice fades: dyes "have the unfortunate habit of fading" (NBS, p. 7), fine for children's pictures. Invisible ink: fresh milk shows when gently heated (Household Discoveries, p. 420).

🧊 Keeping ink, and bringing back faded writing

  • Mold: "a little alcohol or a few drops of any essential oil or a few cloves will prevent mold"; thin thickened ink with water or strained strong coffee (Household Discoveries, p. 422).
  • Air spoils ink: keep bottles nearly full, corked and dark (NBS, pp. 23–24). Make small batches; a two-year supply may end up "too full of sediment" (p. 24).
  • Freezing: a little alcohol prevents it (Household Discoveries, p. 422). Inks frozen solid at about 0 °F came back when thawed and shaken, and no bottle broke; they were about ⅚ full (NBS, p. 25; arithmetic). Leave headroom and keep ink indoors in frost (rule of thumb).

Restoring faded iron-gall writing. It leaves rust in the strokes, so it can be brought back (faded dye ink leaves "literally nothing behind", NBS, pp. 32–33). "A 2- or 3-percent solution of tannic acid", or gall tea, turns the rust black: "by far the best treatment" (p. 33; Household Discoveries, p. 422).

  1. Brush it on softly, or trace the letters with a pen dipped in it; rinse briefly in clean water and dry, or the whole sheet darkens in time (p. 33).
  2. Try a corner first. For a treasured paper, think twice: NBS advised against treating the Declaration of Independence (p. 34).

🪶 Pens: reed, quill and brush

Reed or bamboo pen (VITA, p. 360):

  1. Take dry bamboo or stiff reed about 6 in × ⅜ in. Whittle one end to width and shave it thin so it flexes, keeping the tip on the hard outer side.
  2. Cut the tip straight across, then shape it "by gently writing on the sandpaper with the dry pen".
  3. For an ink hole, rest the knife point at least ⅛ in up from the tip and twist to drill a hole about 3/32 in.
  4. For a reservoir pen, bind a thin bamboo cover plate on with a rubber band or fine wire (p. 361).

Quill pen (rule of thumb; no book here shows how): take a big goose or turkey wing feather; soak the barrel, harden it in hot sand for a minute, strip the lower barbs and scrape off the outer skin and inner pith. Cut a long scoop on the underside, a short slit up the point, and trim the tip square and thin. Recut it as it wears.

Brush: India ink "applies well with a brush" (Henley's, p. 408). A gold nib "cannot be rusted" by any ink (NBS, p. 18).

✏️ Pencils, charcoal, chalk and slates

  • Hoard pencils. An 1871 traveler gave up ink (it dries, clogs and corrodes pens) and "trusted simply to the powers of a good HH. pencil" (Shifts and Expedients, pp. 31–32). Pencil "lead" is graphite and clay, "not lead (the metal)" (Kuphaldt, p. 97).
  • Don't make real lead pencils from melted lead in reeds, as an old book does (Shifts, p. 217). Lead fumes poison: never melt lead indoors, and keep children away (ODFW, p. 27).
  • Sharpener: a block with coarse sandpaper on one side and fine on the other (Household Discoveries, p. 62).
  • Charcoal: a burnt stick marks well enough to lay out a tepee (Seton, p. 467). For drawing sticks, char peeled willow twigs in a tin with a nail hole, like char cloth ("Fire and light without stores" in the Make-Do guide), in the stove during fire season (rule of thumb).
  • Soapstone, chalk and slate pencils mark iron (ICS, p. 55). Stop smudging with "a light wash of gum of any kind, diluted varnish, or even milk" (Leland, p. 259).

Slates save paper. Children learned their tables "upon their slates or on the blackboard" (Ray's Primary, p. 11); a "stick or chalk board" is a toddler's toy (WHO/UNICEF, p. 2); a kitchen slate keeps the list of supplies running low (Household Discoveries, p. 69).

  • Blackboard paint: 1 lb shellac in 1 gal alcohol, with 4 oz each of fine lampblack, powdered emery and ultramarine (Henley's, p. 491). Lacking those, a smooth dark board or flat stone takes chalk or soapstone (rule of thumb).
  • Wax tablet: a shallow tray of beeswax, written on with a point and smoothed to erase (rule of thumb); beeswax takes "any sharp-pointed instrument" (Household Discoveries, p. 421).
  • Birch bark: no book here covers it, and paper birch is not a valley tree (rule of thumb). Smooth bark or leaves take scratched letters, as India's palm leaves did (Shifts, p. 504).

📚 Keeping paper and books from mold

  • Damp breeds "molds and fungi". Keep books on "open, airy, well-lighted shelves"; after a wet spell wipe the covers and set them "in the sun or before a fire for a few hours"; camphor, wrapped in paper, in closed boxes (Henley's, p. 127). In our wet winters, check monthly (rule of thumb).
  • Light and damp age writing; mold destroys "both ink and paper" (NBS, p. 27). Store records dry and dark, off the floor and away from outside walls (rule of thumb).
  • Insects: oil of lavender or cedar on the shelves; silverfish "loves starch" (Household Discoveries, p. 59).
  • Records that must last: soot ink or balanced iron-gall ink on rag paper, never berry or dye ink (NBS, pp. 7, 37).

🧻 Toilet paper alternatives: water, cloth, leaves, cobs

There are two ways: wash ("typically … 0.5–3 litres", about 2 cups to 3 quarts) or wipe with "paper, leaves, corncobs, rags or stones" (Compendium, p. 10; arithmetic). Agree as a household what you'll use, and plan a supply that lasts (Sphere, p. 143).

Water: pour from a squeeze bottle or jug with one hand and wash with the other, then wash your hands with soap (rule of thumb). The wash water carries feces: keep it out of the urine bucket; small amounts may soak into the soil (EcoSanRes, p. 38). Pour-flush toilets suit water-washers (Hesperian ch. 7, p. 35).

Cloth wipes ("family cloth") belong to one person each: shared towels spread disease (Ship's Medicine Chest, p. 153; pink eye, Where There Is No Doctor, ch. 16, p. 3). Cut soft cotton (flannel, old T-shirts) 6–8 inches square, a day's worth plus spares each; many families use cloth for urine only (rule of thumb). Used cloths go in a lidded bucket ("Toilet paper alternatives" in the Make-Do guide).

Wash them like cloth diapers:

  1. Rinse in cold water, then wash with soap ("Menstrual pads and diapers" in the Make-Do guide).
  2. Wash above 140 °F, or soak in 0.05% bleach (about 2 Tbsp of 6% bleach per gallon): "Dishes, laundry and periods" and the bleach-strength table in the Sanitation guide.
  3. A little vinegar in the last rinse, as for diapers (Where There Is No Doctor, ch. 15, p. 23). Dry in full sun.
  4. Wash water goes to the latrine or wastewater system, not the garden (Oregon DEQ, p. 12), and never into a creek. Wash a sick person's cloths separately, with bleach (rule of thumb).

Leaves: no book in the library recommends any particular leaf for wiping. Leaves are a normal choice (Compendium, p. 29), but which leaf is up to you. Rub a new one on your inner arm and wait a day before trusting it (rule of thumb).

  • ⚠️ Poison oak: "leaves of three, let it be"; vine or shrub, "all over low elevation in western Oregon"; "the roots and stems also contain the oil" (OSU EM 9184, p. 24). "All parts, at all times of the year" cause the rash, bare winter stems too (FM 3-05.70, p. 514). Rash care: Injuries guide.
  • ⚠️ Stinging nettle grows on moist forest floors and riverbanks; its hairs sting "for hours or cause blisters" (OSU EM 9373, p. 2).
  • Thimbleberry (rule of thumb): big soft leaves on "a thornless, wideleaved shrub, 3 to 8 feet high, with showy white flowers" (Chesnut, p. 369), in forest shade (OSU EC 1306, p. 4).
  • Mullein (rule of thumb): first-year rosettes of "wooly basal leaves" on roadsides and dry open ground (USFS PNW-GTR-513, p. 131); gentle enough to put on wounds (AFH 10-644, p. 90), but the hairs bother some people (rule of thumb).

Other wipes: dried corncobs (Compendium, p. 10); paper scraps crumpled soft (rule of thumb); stones, kept in a separate pile, not in a dry toilet (EcoSanRes, p. 38). Sore bottom from diarrhea: petroleum jelly or zinc oxide cream after each stool (Where Women Have No Doctor, ch. 17, p. 18).

🧼 Wiping, handwashing and children

  • Wipe from front to back: "Wiping forward can spread germs, amebas, or worms into the urinary opening and vagina" (Where There Is No Doctor, ch. 18, p. 10; Where Women Have No Doctor, ch. 10, p. 7).
  • Teach children "to wipe or wash carefully, and to wash their hands after using the toilet. Girls especially should be taught to wipe from front to back"; wash babies after they defecate (Hesperian ch. 7, p. 11).
  • Wash hands after the toilet and after cleaning up anyone else (OSU, p. 4), with soap "or an alternative such as ash" (Sphere, p. 143). Put a tippy tap by every toilet ("Handwashing and water for hygiene" in the Sanitation guide).

🧣 Handkerchiefs, towels and reusing paper

  • Handkerchiefs, not tissues. Someone with a cold "should cover his nose and mouth when he coughs or sneezes, and wash his hands often", and should not blow but "just wipe" the nose (Where There Is No Doctor, ch. 13, p. 13). Each person keeps their own, washed hot like cloth wipes (rule of thumb).
  • Flour sacks make dish towels and dishcloths (Household Discoveries, p. 68); hem old sheets into napkins (rule of thumb).
  • Paper bags wrap things, then wipe up milk or grease and go in the fire; waxed box liners wrap cheese and lunches (p. 67). Newspaper goes under carpets, wraps woolens against moths and covers plants on frosty nights (p. 68); damp, it polishes windows (p. 275).
  • Paper too far gone: shred it for the bucket toilet, or burn clean paper in the cooking fire, never in the open June to October ("Garbage, sickroom and birth waste" in the Sanitation guide).

⚠️ Old advice in the library to ignore

Book says Do this instead
"5 grains of corrosive sublimate" (mercury) in ink "to prevent mold" (Household Discoveries, p. 417; also pp. 418–419) A few cloves, a little alcohol or essential oil (p. 422)
Corrosive sublimate in paste (Lee's, p. 204) and book varnish (Henley's, p. 127) The starch paste on the same Lee's page; dry, airy shelves
Bichromate of potash in logwood inks (Household Discoveries, p. 418; Lee's, p. 59) Iron-gall or soot ink; logwood-chrome inks "offer no advantage" (NBS, p. 37). Chromium causes cancer (Clothing & Leather guide)
Carbolic acid (phenol), hydrochloric and sulfuric acid in the government formulas (NBS, pp. 13, 16) Leave them out; sulfuric acid "is extremely harmful to paper" (p. 35)
Oxalic-acid blotting paper to lift ink (Henley's, p. 505), and as ink eraser (NBS, p. 36) It is poisonous; tartaric plus citric acid is "much safer", and lemon juice lifts iron rust (same page)
Nitrate of silver marking inks (Household Discoveries, p. 419) Stains and burns skin (rule of thumb); use soot ink
Pencils and inscription plates of melted lead (Shifts, p. 217) Graphite, charcoal, chalk or slate; lead fumes poison (ODFW, p. 27)
Glass crayons of copper sulfate (Henley's, p. 376) and red lead Plain chalk or soapstone (rule of thumb)
Ammonium sulfide fumes to restore writing (NBS, p. 33) Tannin or gall tea (same page)
Chlorine bleaching (Watt, ch. IX, p. 107) and sulfuric-acid "parchment paper" (Watt, p. 199) Keep homemade paper off-white; use waxed paper for jar covers (rule of thumb)
