🫙 Preserving Food Without a Freezer

How to keep a garden, a deer or a run of salmon through the year with no power: canning, jars and lids when the stores are gone, jam, pickles and sauerkraut, drying, jerky and pemmican, smoking and salting fish, curing meat, and keeping lard, eggs and vinegar. The books are in Food-Storage\ (Canning, Curing-Smoking, Drying, Fermenting-Eggs-Vinegar), the USDA canning guides in Gardening-Food\, game and fish canning in Hunting\ and Fishing\, and vinegar, lard and pork in Homestead-Skills\Food-Crafts-Tools. Cellar storage is in the Root Cellar guide; how much to put up is in the Food Security guide.

⚠️ Rules that prevent botulism and poisoning

  • You can't see, smell or taste botulism. Meat, fish, poultry, milk, beans and every vegetable except most tomatoes are low-acid. They go in jars only in a pressure canner. There is no tested way to can them without one, so dry, ferment, salt or cellar them instead.
  • Use a tested recipe, set for our elevation (about 1,300 ft): the 15-lb weight or 11 lb on a dial for pressure canning; water-bath times plus 5 minutes.
  • Boil home-canned meat and vegetables 11 minutes before anyone tastes them.
  • Never taste a suspect jar. Bulging lid, leaks, spurting, bad smell or mold: detoxify it and throw it out. Low-acid jars can be deadly with no signs at all.
  • Weigh curing nitrite exactly. Too much is poison.
  • Smoke and drying don't cook food. Cold-smoked fish is raw. Jerky must reach 160 °F.

☠️ Botulism: why low-acid food needs a pressure canner

Clostridium botulinum spores are on most fresh food. Sealed in a jar of moist, low-acid food at 40–120 °F with no air, they can make a deadly toxin in 3–4 days (USDA Guide 1, p. 6). The food can look, smell and taste normal (PNW 361, p. 2).

Acid foods (pH 4.6 or lower) Low-acid foods
Fruit, jam, pickles, sauerkraut, tomatoes with acid added Meat, fish, poultry, milk, beans, every fresh vegetable, most soups and stews
Boiling-water or steam canner Pressure canner only

The spores die only at 240–250 °F, which takes a pressure canner. A boiling-water canner would need 7–11 hours, and no such times have been tested (USDA Guide 1, p. 8).

Signs of botulism, usually 18–36 hours after eating: double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, dry mouth and weakness, then paralysis spreading down to the breathing muscles (FDA Bad Bug Book, p. 110). Bad cases need weeks on a breathing machine, so prevention is everything. No honey for babies under 12 months (p. 111).

Boil before eating: 10 minutes plus 1 minute per 1,000 ft above 1,000 ft, so 11 minutes here, timed from a full boil. This destroys the toxin, not the spores (PNW 361, p. 2).

No pressure canner? Don't can low-acid food at all. Keep beans, corn and grain dry and roots in the cellar; ferment or salt vegetables; jerk, cure or salt meat; salt, dry, or smoke-and-can fish; and keep animals alive until you need them (general practice).

Canned fruit, tomatoes, vegetables and meat need no sugar or salt for safety, only flavor (USDA Guide 1, p. 31). Jam, pickles and ferments do.

Tomatoes need added acid, even in a pressure canner: per quart, 2 Tbsp bottled lemon juice or ½ tsp citric acid (half that per pint). Never can tomatoes from frost-killed vines, which matters after our October frosts (USDA Guide 3, p. 5). Stockpile citric acid; it keeps for years dry. When it's gone, sun-dry tomatoes in August (general practice).

♨️ Running a pressure canner off-grid

Choose a weighted-gauge canner. It never needs testing. A dial gauge must be tested yearly; one reading high gives unsafe food (PNW 421, p. 5). The canner must hold at least 4 quart jars.

Every batch (USDA Guide 1, pp. 20–22; PNW 421, p. 9):

  1. 2–3 inches of hot water, or 4–5 inches for fish and other long processes. Jars on the rack.
  2. Lock the lid, weight off. Heat until steam pours out, then vent a full 10 minutes. Put the weight on.
  3. Start timing when the weight jiggles. If pressure ever drops below the target, bring it back up and start the whole time over.
  4. Turn off the heat and let it cool by itself. Never cool it with water. At zero pressure, take off the weight, wait 10 minutes, and open the lid away from your face.

On a wood stove, campfire or outdoor burner there is no tested method. Canner makers forbid outdoor LP burners and any burner over 12,000 BTU. In Utah State tests, canners boiled dry within minutes and warped, and cold outdoor air breaks jars and spoils seals (WSU flyer, p. 1). Never "can" in any oven (PNW 361, p. 2).

If a wood range is your only heat (general practice: untested, so the risk is yours):

  • Water-bath canning works if you hold a full rolling boil and restart the timer whenever it stops (USDA Guide 1, p. 18).
  • For pressure canning, practice first with jars of plain water. Use 4–5 inches of water. Control heat by sliding the canner between hotter and cooler parts of the stovetop, not by adding wood. Restart the timer every time pressure dips. Never walk away.
  • If you can't hold steady pressure, don't can low-acid food.

Care (PNW 421, pp. 6–8, 12): stock spare gaskets and safety (overpressure) plugs now. Replace a gasket that's worn, stretched or hardened. Small steam leaks are fine if pressure holds; add extra water. Clean the vent with a string. Store it dry, lid upside down, not locked.

If this goes wrong Do this
Steam pouring out at the lid Replace the gasket
Jars under-processed (boiled dry, lost the time) Reprocess within 24 hours with new lids for the full time, or throw them out (PNW 450, p. 4)
A jar didn't seal New lid and reprocess within 24 hours, or keep it cold and eat it within days (USDA Guide 1, p. 26)

🥫 Jars, lids, storage and spoilage

  • Mason jars last for years; only the flat lid must be new. Throw out chipped jars (PNW 361, p. 3). Mayonnaise-type jars are for acid foods in a water bath only, never a pressure canner (USDA Guide 1, p. 13).
  • One-time metal lids shouldn't be reused. Opening warps them and the sealing compound is spent. Unused lids keep about 5 years, so stockpile them. Buy from the maker; many other sellers' lids were poor in 2021 (FN2028, p. 1).
  • Reusable plastic lids with rubber rings (the Tattler type) sealed safely in University of Georgia research. Follow the maker's directions exactly, which may include tightening the band as soon as the jar comes out (FN2028, p. 1).
  • If you must reuse a one-time lid (general practice, not tested): only on high-acid jars (jam, fruit, pickles, kraut), where failure shows as mold or fizzing, not hidden toxin. Never on low-acid food.

Test seals after 12–24 hours, bands off. Press the middle: if it springs back, it's not sealed. Tap with a teaspoon: a ringing note means sealed; a dull one means not (USDA Guide 1, p. 25).

Store cool, dark and dry, 50–70 °F, never above 95 °F; use within 2 years (p. 26). Attics, sheds and garages here pass 95 °F in summer, so use the cellar or under the house (general practice).

Throw it out if the lid is loose or bulging, food has streaked down the jar, bubbles rise, the liquid is cloudy or spurts, it smells bad, or there's mold (USDA Guide 1, pp. 26–27). Also badly dented, bulging, rusty or leaking store cans (USU, p. 40).

Getting rid of a suspect jar (USDA Guide 1, p. 27):

  1. Gloves on. Lay jars and lids on their sides in a big pot, cover with water, and boil 30 minutes. Then discard.
  2. Wipe every surface with 1 part bleach to 5 parts water, leave 30 minutes, and do it again.

🍑 Water-bath and steam canning; jam without pectin

Water bath (USDA Guide 1, p. 18): keep 1–2 inches of water over the jars. Start timing at a hard boil; if the boil stops, start over. At the end, take the lid off, wait 5 minutes, lift the jars out, and leave them alone 12–24 hours.

Steam canner (saves fuel and water): approved for acid foods only, using a tested boiling-water recipe. The total time must be 45 minutes or less. Vent until a full column of steam shows, and never open it to add water (K-State MF3241, p. 2).

Jam and jelly without bought pectin:

  • Apples, crab apples, gooseberries, some plums and grapes have their own pectin; strawberries, cherries and blueberries don't. For jelly, use ¾ ripe and ¼ under-ripe fruit (USDA Guide 1, p. 29).
  • Berry jam: 4 cups crushed berries and 4 cups sugar makes 3–4 half-pints. Boil hard to 217–218 °F, or until two drops run together off a cool spoon. Process 10 minutes here (USDA Guide 7, p. 6; PNW 652, p. 26). Feral blackberries ripen by the bucketful in July and August (general practice).
  • Don't cut the sugar. Too little gives a weak gel, mold and yeast (USDA Guide 1, p. 30).

🥒 Pickles, sauerkraut and other ferments

Vinegar pickles Fermented pickles and kraut
Needs Store 5% vinegar, tested recipe Salt only
Homemade vinegar? No. Its strength is unknown Not needed
Long term Ends when vinegar runs out The way to go (general practice)

Never change a tested recipe's amounts, and never cut the salt in a ferment (USDA Guide 1, p. 28).

Sauerkraut (USDA Guide 6, pp. 6–8):

  • 25 lb cabbage to ¾ cup canning salt (3 Tbsp per 5 lb), within 24–48 hours of harvest. A 1-gallon crock per 5 lb.
  • Weight it with a sealed bag of brine (3 quarts water + 4½ Tbsp salt), so a leak won't weaken the brine. Keep it under brine; top up with 1½ Tbsp salt per quart of water.
  • 3–4 weeks at 70–75 °F, or 5–6 weeks at 60–65 °F. Below 60 °F it may not ferment; above 75 °F it goes soft. October–November rooms here sit near 60–65 °F (general practice).
  • To can it: hot pack, 15 minutes for pints, 20 for quarts here.

Fermented dill pickles, per gallon: 4 lb of 4-inch cucumbers, ½ cup salt, ¼ cup 5% vinegar, 8 cups water, dill. 3–4 weeks at 70–75 °F; skim scum often. Soft, slimy or bad-smelling: throw them out (USDA Guide 6, p. 7).

Any vegetable (K-State MF3491, p. 2): dry-salt shredded vegetables with 1 Tbsp non-iodized salt per 2 lb, or brine chunks in 1 tsp salt per cup of water (2 tsp for cucumbers or peppers). Keep them weighted under brine at 60–70 °F.

Heavy salting, no fermenting (1918): 15–25% brine keeps beans, beets, corn, chard and cabbage until spring. Soak in fresh water to remove the salt, then cook (1918 bulletin, p. 7).

☀️ Drying in our dry summers

Trays and solar dryers are in the Root Cellar guide.

  • Sun drying needs about 90 °F, low humidity and clean air. A solar dryer is dependable with 3–5 days in a row near 95 °F, which is an ordinary July to September here (PNW 651, p. 16).
  • Not in wildfire smoke. Bring trays in when smoke settles in the valley (general practice).
  • Dries well: apples, pears, plums, cherries, peaches, grapes, huckleberries. Not blackberries.
  • No sulfite dips for anyone with asthma (p. 18).
  • Fruit leather from windfalls: heat the purée to 160 °F first (p. 20).
  • Done? Pack it loosely in covered jars 2–4 days, shaking daily. Moisture on the lid means dry it more (p. 18).
  • Kill insect eggs before storing: 160 °F for 30 minutes (general practice).

📚 Go deeper: PNW 651 Drying Project Manual — fruit by fruit; Wikipedia: Food drying.

🥩 Jerky and pemmican

Safe jerky (PNW 632):

  1. Slice lean meat into strips.
  2. Bring it to 160 °F (poultry 165 °F): dip strips 1½–2 minutes in simmering brine before drying (p. 9), or heat dried strips 10 minutes at 275 °F (p. 17).
  3. Dry at 145–155 °F, checked with a thermometer, until a cooled strip cracks but doesn't snap (p. 18).
  4. Keep it sealed; it's best within 1–2 months (p. 21).

Don't sun-dry meat (USU, p. 59). Bear and cougar carry trichinosis; ten people caught it from cougar jerky (PNW 632, p. 8).

Pemmican: lean meat dried by sun, wind or smoke (not salt-cured), pounded, and mixed with hot rendered fat. About 6 lb of meat makes 1 lb, and it keeps for years, depending on the fat (Special Forces handbook, p. 174). There's no modern tested recipe, so (general practice): do the 160 °F step, then dry the meat brittle; render the fat until it stops bubbling; use about equal weights of meat and fat; firm tallow (deer, elk, beef) keeps better than lard; fully dry any berries. Store it cool, dark and airtight; throw it out if rancid or sour.

🐟 Fish: smoking, canning and salting

Cold smoke Hot smoke
Heat Smokehouse under 80–90 °F 150 °F inside the fish (160 °F better) for 30+ minutes
Cooked? No. It's raw Yes
Keeps Only if heavily salted and long-smoked About 2 weeks, refrigerated

Hot smoking (PNW 238, pp. 1–3): brine 2–3 lb of fish in 1 cup salt to 7 cups water for about an hour; let a tacky skin (pellicle) form; smoke with hardwood, never fir, pine or cedar. Here, use red alder, bigleaf maple, oak or fruitwood (general practice).

Smoke alone doesn't preserve. With no refrigerator, can it (PNW 450, pp. 1–3):

  1. Smoke lightly, up to 2 hours, to about 10% weight loss (weigh a piece before and after). Don't eat it yet.
  2. Can it the same day: pints, no liquid. This recipe uses 4 quarts of cool water in the canner; don't heat it or use less (p. 3). Vent 10 minutes, then 110 minutes at the 15-lb weight or 11-lb dial.

The old way, no canner: 24 hours of cold smoke keeps fish about 2 weeks; longer keeping takes 5+ days of smoke at or below 90 °F (Fishery Leaflet 18, p. 15). Our summers are too hot, so cold-smoke October to April, when fall Chinook and winter steelhead run. In fire season, smoke only on bare ground with water at hand (general practice).

Salting fish (Fishery Leaflet 18, pp. 2–7):

  • Bleed, gut and clean the fish well. To hold it a day with no ice, rub in about 1 Tbsp salt per ¾ lb and keep it under wet burlap in the shade. Rolled in salt, it keeps about 10 days; soak it 10 hours before cooking.
  • Dry salting keeps longer than brine and suits warm weather. Brined fish keeps up to 9 months if you change the brine at 3 months. Dried salt fish is done when a thumb leaves no dent in the thick part.
  • No modern guide tests long-term salting of fatty salmon. Treat it as an old-time method and cook it well.

🥓 Curing pork and venison

Cold is the key: 36–40 °F for the whole cure. Warmer risks spoilage; colder slows the salt (USDA Leaflet 273, p. 5), and curing stops below 34 °F (NDSU, p. 4). Cure December to early February, when nights here fall to the low 30s (general practice). A ham is in trouble only if its inside stays above 40–45 °F for 3–4 days running (Kentucky, p. 3).

Cure (weigh it) Amounts Time Source
Country ham 8 lb salt + 2 lb sugar per 100 lb. Never more sugar than salt 2 days per lb Kentucky, p. 2
Nitrite dry cure 8 lb salt, 3 lb sugar, 2 oz sodium nitrate, ½ oz sodium nitrite. 1 oz mix per lb of meat (1½ oz over 20 lb). Bacon: half the nitrite and nitrate 7 days per inch MSU P2648, p. 1
Venison Per 100 lb: 6 lb salt, 3 lb sugar, and 3 oz nitrate or 1 oz nitrite Brine: 3 days per lb at 38 °F NDSU, p. 4
Plain salt 1 part salt to 3 parts meat, strips 1 inch thick or less, weighted in a tub Cook it thoroughly CVM, p. 90
  • "Too much nitrate and nitrite can be toxic" (MSU P2648, p. 1). Pink premixes: Cure #1 (6.25% nitrite) for short cures, Cure #2 (with nitrate) for long dry cures. Follow the label, commonly 1 level tsp Cure #1 per 5 lb of meat, and store it away from table salt and children (general practice).
  • A sweet-pickle brine should just float an egg. If it turns ropy, take the meat out and boil or replace the brine (NDSU, p. 4).
  • Barrel-salted meat (12–18% salt) keeps 3–4 months at room temperature (Practical Action, p. 4). Salt doesn't kill all germs or parasites (CVM, p. 91).

Smoke and store:

  • Smoke cured pork with hardwood (never pine) at 100–120 °F (Leaflet 273, p. 8). Cold smoke stays under 100 °F (70–80 °F best) for 1–5 days; spoilage bacteria grow fast at 80–110 °F (MSU P2648, p. 2).
  • In spring, hold hams at 50–60 °F so the salt evens out (Kentucky, p. 5).
  • Smoked country hams keep a year or more in a dry, dark, airy place; trim off surface mold. Eat bacon by early summer (USDA FB 1186, p. 31).
  • Throw it out if it smells sour at the bone or has wet patches and holes through the meat (Special Forces handbook, p. 173).

🥚 Lard, confit, eggs and vinegar

  • Lard: render slowly, never above 240–255 °F. The fuller the rendering, the less water and the better it keeps (Minnesota, p. 3). Steps are in Food From Scratch.
  • ⚠️ Confit and potted meat (meat sealed under lard) are airless and low-acid, exactly what botulism needs. No method is tested. Use them only short-term in a cold winter cellar, and boil before eating (general practice).
  • Eggs: fresh, clean, uncracked spring eggs, wiped not washed, in 1 quart water glass (sodium silicate) to 9 quarts boiled, cooled water, 2 inches over them, or in clear limewater (2 lb lime in 5 gallons boiled water). Prick the shell before boiling (USDA 1935, pp. 3–4). USDA says 6–9 months, FAO only 1–6 (FAO, p. 83); plan on the shorter.
  • Vinegar: yeast turns fruit juice to alcohol in 4–6 days. Then add 1 part unpasteurized vinegar to 4 parts juice, leave it open under cheesecloth at 70 °F or warmer, and don't disturb the "mother." It takes 3–6 months (USDA FB 1424, pp. 9–12). Only a titration test proves its strength (p. 30), so use it for cooking, never for canned pickles.

⚠️ Old advice in the library to ignore

  • Open-kettle canning: jars "filled to overflowing," turned upside down, never processed, even vegetables (Henley, p. 605; Household Discoveries, p. 542, p. 544), and cotton batting or mutton suet "as if hermetically sealed" (p. 549). Instead: process every jar (USDA Guide 1, p. 10).
  • Oven canning: jars can seal and explode, and the food never gets hot enough (FB 1186, p. 37). Instead: a pressure canner.
  • Paraffin seals on jam (mold toxins; USDA Guide 1, p. 30), on kraut fermented at 86 °F (FB 1438, p. 14) and on smoked fish (Fishery Leaflet 18, p. 2). Instead: process jam; ferment at 60–75 °F under brine.
  • Borax, boric acid, salicylic acid (the folk "aspirin in the jar" trick) and salts up to 40% saltpeter (Henley, p. 363). Poisons. Instead: heat, acid and salt by a tested recipe.
  • "10:1 table salt and saltpeter," about ten times modern levels (Special Forces handbook, p. 174). Instead: the MSU amounts or a premix label.
  • Hot-smoking fish in 130–150 °F air for 2–3 hours (Fishery Leaflet 18, p. 14). Instead: 150–160 °F inside the fish for 30 minutes (PNW 238, p. 2).
  • Jerky dried at 120 °F with no heat step, "almost indefinitely" keeping (NDSU, p. 5), and sun-dried jerky (Special Forces handbook, p. 174). Instead: the 160 °F step.
  • Half-fried chops sealed under lard "until required" (Household Discoveries, p. 589). Instead: cold-cellar winter use only, boiled, or pressure-can the meat.
  • "Steam canners are not currently recommended" (USDA Guide 1, p. 10) is out of date. They're approved for acid foods (K-State MF3241, p. 2).
