☢️ Nuclear Fallout Guide

What to do if there is a nuclear detonation or a radiation release: how to shelter, how long, how to protect food, water and animals, how to clean up, and when potassium iodide (KI) helps. The full manuals — shelter plans, the homemade KFM fallout meter, farm and livestock guides — are in Nuclear-Radiation\.

The three rules

  1. Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned. Most deaths from fallout are avoidable just by being behind enough mass in the first day or two.
  2. Fallout is dust. It hurts you from what lands on and near you, so put mass and distance between you and the roof, walls and ground outside.
  3. Time is on your side. Radiation from fallout drops fast — 90% in the first 7 hours, 99% in 2 days.

⚡ The first minutes

  • If you see a flash: drop behind anything solid, face down, cover your head, and don't look at it. The blast wave can arrive many seconds later — stay down until it passes.
  • Fallout near a blast can start arriving in 15–30 minutes; 100–200 miles downwind, often 5–10 hours or more. Use the time before it arrives to put out small fires, bring people, pets and water inside, and move to the best shelter you can reach. (FEMA Protection in the Nuclear Age, H-20)
  • Officials will tell everyone within about 50 miles to shelter until they know where the fallout is going. (FEMA Planning Guidance, 3rd ed.)

🏠 Where to shelter

Best to worst: a basement (especially a corner below ground level) → the middle floors and center of a large concrete or brick building → a crawl space or interior room of a house → a vehicle (poor).

Most homes in the Illinois Valley are one-story wood frame with no basement. FEMA says these, and vehicles, are not adequate shelter on their own. Plan now: a nearby building with a basement or thick masonry, a crawl-space shelter you can shield, or an outdoor trench shelter (plans in MP-79, In Time of Emergency, and Nuclear War Survival Skills Appendix A).

  • Inside any building: get as far from the roof and outside walls as you can, away from windows and doors. Close and lock windows, turn off fans, A/C and forced-air heat, close fireplace dampers. (CDC Where to Go)
  • Stuck in a poor shelter? If a good one is under 15 minutes away, move within about 30 minutes of the blast, before heavy fallout arrives. If you're already in a moderate shelter, stay 1–2 hours, then move. (FEMA Planning Guidance)

Adding shielding — each of these stops about as much as 4 inches of concrete (FEMA H-20):

Material Thickness
Brick 5–6 in
Sand or gravel 6 in
Packed earth 7 in
Hollow concrete block (sand-filled) 8 in (6 in)
Water 10 in
Books or magazines 14 in
Wood (firewood stack) 18 in

Pile it on the floor above you and around the walls of your shelter spot: earth-filled drawers and boxes, water jugs, firewood, books, heavy furniture. Every extra ~4 inches of packed earth roughly halves what gets through — about 3 feet of earth cuts it around 1,000×. Crawl space: shelter under the center of the house, ring yourself with blocks or earth-filled containers, and pile mass on the floor above.

Ventilation: a crowded, sealed shelter gets dangerously hot in warm weather. Kearny's homemade air pump (KAP) — plans in Nuclear War Survival Skills and the ORNL Expedient Shelter Handbook — moves air without bringing in fallout dust.

⏳ How long to stay in

The 7-10 rule: every 7× increase in time after the blast cuts the radiation 10× (once the fallout has finished falling).

Time after blast Radiation level (example)
1 hour 1,000 R/hr
7 hours 100 R/hr
2 days 10 R/hr
2 weeks 1 R/hr
  • Stay sheltered at least 12–24 hours (FEMA). In heavy fallout areas it can be several days to two weeks, with only short trips out once levels drop (H-20).
  • Without a meter, listen for official word. With a meter, you can make your own decisions — see below.

🧼 Cleaning yourself up (decontamination)

(CDC; EPA Protective Action Q&A)

  • Taking off your outer layer of clothes removes up to 90% of the fallout on you. Do it before you enter the shelter if you can. Bag the clothes and put them away from people and pets.
  • Shower with soap and shampoo — no conditioner (it glues particles to hair). Don't scrub hard or scratch the skin; keep cuts covered.
  • No shower? Wipe exposed skin, blow your nose, wipe eyelids, ears and face with a damp cloth.
  • Clothes from closed drawers and closets are fine to wear.
  • Pets: bring them in. Wash with soap or shampoo and water, wearing gloves and a dust mask, rinse completely, then wash yourself.
  • Fallout looks like sand, ash or grit. Brush or hose it off roofs and away from the house after levels drop; wear a dust mask, gloves and a hat, and wash afterward.

💊 Potassium iodide (KI)

  • KI only protects the thyroid, and only from radioactive iodine. It does nothing against other fallout. Take it only when health officials say to — or, with no officials, when you have reason to believe fresh fallout or a reactor plume is arriving. Table salt and iodine tincture are not substitutes (tincture is poisonous to swallow). (CDC; FDA)
  • One dose protects about 24 hours. It works best just before or as the plume arrives and still helps 3–4 hours after. Avoid repeat doses for newborns and pregnant or breastfeeding women. (FDA)

FDA dose by age (one dose per day):

Who Dose
Adults over 18 (including pregnant or breastfeeding) 130 mg
Ages 12–18 (130 mg if 154 lb or more) 65 mg
Ages 3–12 65 mg
1 month – 3 years 32 mg
Birth – 1 month 16 mg

Adults over 40 need it only for a very large expected exposure. To make liquid doses for small children from tablets, use the two FDA sheets in Nuclear-Radiation\Government-Guidance.

🥫 Food and water

(EPA Protective Action Q&A; FDA; Nuclear War Survival Skills Ch. 8)

  • Food that was indoors is fine. Sealed cans, jars and bags that were outside are fine after wiping the outside. Stored grain and canned food have no radioactive-iodine risk.
  • Don't eat garden produce or anything left uncovered outdoors until levels have dropped and it's declared clear — then wash and peel it.
  • Don't drink fresh milk from animals grazing fallout-covered pasture for the first weeks (radioactive iodine concentrates in milk). Milk made into cheese and aged two months or more is fine — the radioactive iodine decays away.
  • Water: bottled and sealed water is certain. Covered wells, tanks and cisterns are the best sources; deep wells are well protected. Tap water is fine for washing.
  • Boiling does not remove radioactivity. To clean doubtful water: stir in clay soil, let it settle several hours, and pour off the clear water — or filter it through a bucket of packed earth. Both remove most fallout particles. Then disinfect as usual.
  • Cover rain barrels, tanks and well heads ahead of time.

🐄 Livestock and gardens

(USDA FB 2107, PA-516; Oregon ODOE)

  • Shelter animals if you can (a barn helps), and take them off pasture. Feed protected feed — hay from inside the stack, covered silage, grain from closed bins.
  • Water from a covered well, tank or flowing spring is best; open pond water clears more slowly but improves quickly.
  • Hold off on slaughter, fresh milk and eggs until levels drop.
  • Gardens: stop harvesting; after levels drop, wash and peel produce. Fallout on soil decays and is diluted by digging it in.

📟 Measuring radiation

  • A meter turns guessing into decisions — when to leave the shelter, which room is safest, whether the water is clear.
  • KFM (Kearny Fallout Meter): a homemade meter from a can, foil and paper. Build it before you need it. The patterns must print exactly to scale — check against a ruler; OISM warns online copies of the templates aren't fully reliable and recommends the printed book. Full patterns and tables: ORNL-5040 and Nuclear War Survival Skills Appendix C.
  • Old civil-defense meters turn up at sales: the yellow CD V-715 reads high levels (fallout); the CD V-700 is a low-range Geiger counter for checking food, water and people. Manuals and a home-study course are in Nuclear-Radiation\Measuring-Radiation.

📍 Our area

  • The region's only operating nuclear power plant is the Columbia Generating Station at Hanford, Washington — roughly 350 miles from the Illinois Valley, far outside its planning zones. (Oregon ODOE)
  • The more realistic threats here are fallout drifting from detonations elsewhere and fallout carried across the Pacific on west winds. After Chernobyl, radioactive iodine showed up in milk in northwest Oregon. (Nuclear War Survival Skills)
  • OISM, publisher of Nuclear War Survival Skills, is in Cave Junction — the printed edition (with accurate KFM patterns) may be easy to get locally.

The 1987 edition of Nuclear War Survival Skills includes the author's personal view that small radiation doses are healthful; FEMA, EPA and CDC do not accept that. Follow the government guidance in this folder for dose decisions.
