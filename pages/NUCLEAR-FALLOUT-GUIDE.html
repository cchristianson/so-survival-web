☢️ Nuclear Fallout Guide

What to do if there is a nuclear detonation or a radiation release: how to shelter, how long, how to protect food, water and animals, how to clean up, and when potassium iodide (KI) helps. The full manuals — shelter plans, the homemade KFM fallout meter, farm and livestock guides — are in Nuclear-Radiation\.

The three rules

  1. Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned. Most deaths from fallout are avoidable just by being behind enough mass in the first day or two.
  2. Fallout is dust. It hurts you from what lands on and near you, so put mass and distance between you and the roof, walls and ground outside.
  3. Time is on your side. Radiation from fallout drops fast — 90% in the first 7 hours, 99% in 2 days.

⚡ The first minutes

  • If you see a flash: drop behind anything solid, face down, cover your head, and don't look at it — the flash can blind you even when you are too far away for the blast to hurt you (FEMA H-20, p. 14). The blast wave can arrive many seconds later — stay down until it passes. Being outdoors in the first minute can mean burns, cuts from flying glass and broken bones (FEMA Planning Guidance, p. 76). What happens in those seconds: flash, heat and blast drawing; Effects of nuclear explosions, Flash blindness.
  • Fallout near a blast can start arriving in 15–30 minutes; 100–200 miles downwind, often 5–10 hours or more. Use the time before it arrives to put out small fires, bring people, pets and water inside, and move to the best shelter you can reach. (FEMA Protection in the Nuclear Age, H-20, p. 7; how fallout comes down; Nuclear fallout)
  • Officials will tell everyone within about 50 miles to shelter until they know where the fallout is going. (FEMA Planning Guidance, 3rd ed., p. 54)
  • Know the sirens. A 3–5 minute steady blast means "turn on the radio." A 3–5 minute wavering tone (or short blasts repeated) is the attack warning — go to shelter now. (FEMA H-20, p. 12; what they look like: Civil defense siren)

First-hour checklist: get people and pets inside; close doors, windows and blinds; take a few minutes to check the house for fires — stamp out smoldering drapes, throw burning furniture outside (FEMA H-20, p. 26); fill containers and tubs while the taps run (rule of thumb); carry food, water, radio, medicines and the toilet bucket to the shelter spot.

🏠 Where to shelter

Best to worst: a basement (especially a corner below ground level) → the middle floors and center of a large concrete or brick building → a crawl space or interior room of a house → a vehicle (poor). See the FEMA drawing of protection inside different buildings and Fallout shelter.

Most homes in the Illinois Valley are one-story wood frame with no basement. FEMA says these, and vehicles, are not adequate shelter on their own (Planning Guidance, p. 76). Plan now: a nearby building with a basement or thick masonry, a crawl-space shelter you can shield, or an outdoor trench shelter (plans in MP-79, In Time of Emergency, and Nuclear War Survival Skills Appendix A — see the Door-Covered Trench Shelter and Pole-Covered Trench Shelter drawings). Permanent backyard plans: FEMA H-12-1 outside concrete shelter, FEMA H-12-2 aboveground shelter.

  • Inside any building: get as far from the roof and outside walls as you can, away from windows and doors. Close and lock windows, turn off fans, A/C and forced-air heat, close fireplace dampers. (CDC Where to Go; CDC picture: best places inside a building)
  • No need to tape windows or hang plastic sheeting — fallout shelters don't have to be airtight. A building with minor damage still works if it is structurally sound. (EPA Protective Action Q&A, p. 31; Planning Guidance, p. 76)
  • Stuck in a poor shelter? If a good one is under 15 minutes away, move within about 30 minutes of the blast, before heavy fallout arrives. If you're already in a moderate shelter, stay 1–2 hours, then move. (FEMA Planning Guidance, p. 77)

"Adequate" means it cuts your dose 10× or more. Concrete, brick, stone and earth stop a lot; wood, drywall and sheet metal stop little (Planning Guidance, p. 76; EPA dose-reduction diagram by building type).

Adding shielding — each of these stops about as much as 4 inches of concrete (FEMA H-20, p. 22):

Material Thickness
Brick 5–6 in
Sand or gravel 6 in
Packed earth 7 in
Hollow concrete block (sand-filled) 8 in (6 in)
Water 10 in
Books or magazines 14 in
Wood (firewood stack) 18 in

Pile it on the floor above you and around the walls of your shelter spot: earth-filled drawers and boxes, water jugs, firewood, books, heavy furniture. Every extra ~4 inches of packed earth roughly halves what gets through — about 3 feet of earth cuts it around 1,000× (Nuclear War Survival Skills, 1979, p. 23: one "halving thickness" of packed earth is 3.6 in; halving-thickness drawing; Radiation protection). Crawl space: shelter under the center of the house, ring yourself with blocks or earth-filled containers, and pile mass on the floor above.

Quick shelters, step by step (FEMA H-20, pp. 23–25; HS-4, p. 67):

  1. Table shelter: a sturdy table or workbench in the most-underground basement corner, loaded on top and walled around with shielding. Crawl in and block the opening. (drawing of a basement table shelter)
  2. Door-roof shelter: walls of dressers or appliances, a roof of heavy doors propped and piled with shielding. Fill drawers and boxes with earth after they're in place — full ones are too heavy to carry.
  3. Crawl-space shelter: cut an access now. Under the middle of the house, wall a spot with blocks or earth-filled containers up to the joists and pile mass on the floor above. Make sure no heavy appliance sits right above you. Dig down so you can sit up.
  4. Last resort: dig a trench beside a slab house and tunnel no more than 4 feet under the slab; shore it and pile shielding on the slab inside.

Ventilation: a crowded, sealed shelter gets dangerously hot in warm weather. Kearny's homemade air pump (KAP) — plans in Nuclear War Survival Skills and the ORNL Expedient Shelter Handbook — moves air without bringing in fallout dust (KAP swinging in a doorway; photos of homemade KAPs). In a heat wave, plan on about 4 quarts of water and 10 grams of salt per person a day and as few clothes as practical (NWSS 1979, p. 62).

🧰 Stocking the shelter

FEMA says to have at least two weeks of water, food and supplies ready to carry to the shelter (H-20, p. 13; illustrated 1961 supply chart).

Item How much / what kind (FEMA H-20, pp. 29–31)
Water H-20's bare minimum is 1 quart per person per day just to drink; today FEMA advises storing 1 gallon per person per day. Kearny says store 15 gallons per person for two weeks (NWSS 1979, p. 71; storing water in lined bags and pits).
Food Canned or sealed, no cooking needed. Most adults do fine on half their usual amount — not children or pregnant women.
Toilet Bucket with a tight lid, liner bags, a second lidded can, bleach, toilet paper (hose-vented toilet-can; Bucket toilet).
Radio and light Battery radio, flashlight, spare batteries — the radio "may be your only link."
Medicines, babies Two weeks of regular medicines and a first-aid kit; formula or canned milk and diapers.
Tools Shovel, pick, saw, hammer, crowbar, broom, nails, wire (H-20, p. 18).
  • Water hiding in your house: the water heater (20–60 gallons), toilet tanks (not bowls) and the pipes — shut the main valve, open the highest faucet, draw from the lowest (H-20, pp. 31–32). Don't ration drinking water below thirst — nothing is gained by it.
  • Never burn charcoal indoors; any stove used inside needs good ventilation.
  • Toilet routine: a little bleach after each use, lid back on; when you can step out briefly, bury the waste 1–2 feet deep (H-20, p. 33).

⏳ How long to stay in

The 7-10 rule: every 7× increase in time after the blast cuts the radiation 10× (once the fallout has finished falling). (FEMA H-20, p. 8; see the seven-ten rule chart and the decay curve for the first two days)

Time after blast Radiation level (example)
1 hour 1,000 R/hr
7 hours 100 R/hr
2 days 10 R/hr
2 weeks 1 R/hr
  • Stay sheltered at least 12–24 hours unless officials say otherwise or fire, collapse or a medical emergency forces you out (FEMA Planning Guidance, pp. 53, 76). In heavy fallout areas it can be several days to two weeks, with only short trips out once levels drop (H-20, p. 9). USDA adds that where fallout is light or moderate you might be able to come out for part of each day after 2–3 days (PA-515, p. 6).
  • Late fallout decays more slowly where you are — if it arrives 8 hours after the blast, it takes about two days to fall to one-tenth (KFM report ORNL-5040, p. 69).
  • Gritty dust on ledges, cars or walks after an attack = assume fallout (H-20, p. 33).
  • Without a meter, listen for official word. With a meter, you can make your own decisions — see below.

Dose yardsticks — what a short-term whole-body dose does to a group of people (DCPA home-study course, p. 49; Acute radiation syndrome):

Dose received over a short time What to expect
50 R No visible effects
75–100 R Brief nausea the same day in about 1 in 10 people
200 R Up to half get some radiation sickness; 5–10% need care; deaths not expected
450 R Serious sickness in most; about half die within 2–4 weeks
600 R Serious sickness in all; nearly all die within 1–3 weeks

Sources set the line differently: the old civil-defense course says keep short-term doses under 100 R (DCPA, p. 50); today's EPA guideline for emergency workers is 5 rem, up to 25 rem only to save lives (EPA PAG Manual, p. 46). Treat 1 R of fallout as about 1 rem (rule of thumb). Lower is always better — the dose adds up.

🧼 Cleaning yourself up (decontamination)

(CDC Decontamination; EPA Protective Action Q&A, p. 32; pictures: CDC step-by-step sheet, FEMA self-decontamination steps, Radioactive contamination)

  • Taking off your outer layer of clothes removes up to 90% of the fallout on you. Do it before you enter the shelter if you can, and do it gently so dust doesn't shake loose. Bag the clothes and put them away from people and pets.
  • Shower with soap and shampoo — no conditioner (it glues particles to hair). Don't scrub hard or scratch the skin; keep cuts covered. Keep suds out of your mouth and don't swallow the water.
  • No shower? Wipe exposed skin, blow your nose, wipe eyelids, ears and face with a damp cloth.
  • Clothes from closed drawers and closets are fine to wear. No clean clothes? Take off the outer layer, shake or brush it off, put it back on, then rewash your hands and face.
  • A cut or wound with fallout in it: flush with plenty of clean water or saline, or keep it covered (EPA Q&A, p. 33).
  • Visitors: have them strip the outer layer, bag it, and wash before coming in — then they're no danger to you (EPA Q&A, p. 33). Radiation sickness is not contagious (H-20, p. 9).
  • Pets: bring them in. Wash with soap or shampoo and water, wearing gloves and a dust mask, keep your cuts (and theirs) covered, rinse completely, then wash yourself (EPA Q&A, p. 44).
  • Fallout looks like sand, ash or grit (photos of real fallout particles). Brush or hose it off roofs and away from the house after levels drop; wear a dust mask, gloves and a hat, and wash afterward. If you must be out while dust is in the air, cover nose and mouth with a cloth (EPA Q&A, p. 30).

💊 Potassium iodide (KI)

  • KI only protects the thyroid, and only from radioactive iodine. It does nothing against other fallout. Take it only when health officials say to — or, with no officials, when you have reason to believe fresh fallout or a reactor plume is arriving. Table salt and iodine tincture are not substitutes (tincture is poisonous to swallow). Iodine dietary supplements aren't either — use only FDA-approved KI. (CDC How KI Works; FDA, p. 12; CDC picture sheet: how KI fills the thyroid; where the gland is: Thyroid)
  • One dose protects about 24 hours. It works best just before or as the plume arrives and still helps 3–4 hours after. Avoid repeat doses for newborns and pregnant or breastfeeding women. (FDA, pp. 9–11)

FDA dose by age (one dose per day) (FDA 2001, p. 9):

Who Dose 130 mg tablets 65 mg tablets
Adults over 18 (including pregnant or breastfeeding) 130 mg 1 2
Ages 12–18 (130 mg if 154 lb or more) 65 mg ½ 1
Ages 3–12 65 mg ½ 1
1 month – 3 years 32 mg ¼ ½
Birth – 1 month 16 mg ⅛ ¼

Adults over 40 need it only for a very large expected exposure. To make liquid doses for small children from tablets, use the two FDA sheets in Nuclear-Radiation\Government-Guidance.

Making liquid KI for children — the two FDA sheets give the same recipe for one 130 mg tablet or two 65 mg tablets (FDA, one 130 mg tablet; FDA, two 65 mg tablets):

  1. Put the tablet(s) in a small bowl with 4 teaspoons of water. Soak 1 minute.
  2. Crush with the back of a spoon until there are no large pieces.
  3. Stir in 4 teaspoons of milk, chocolate milk, orange juice, cola, formula, raspberry syrup or water. You now have 8 teaspoons of KI liquid.
  4. Give once a day: 19 and older — 8 tsp; 13–18 years and 150 lb or more — 8 tsp; 13–18 under 150 lb — 4 tsp; 4–12 years — 4 tsp; over 1 month to 3 years — 2 tsp; birth to 1 month — 1 tsp.
  5. Keep leftover liquid in the refrigerator; throw it out after 7 days.

(The liquid sheet uses 150 lb for teens; the 2001 FDA guidance says 70 kg, about 154 lb. Either way a near-adult-size teen gets the adult dose.)

  • A nursing mother's KI does not protect her baby — the baby needs its own dose, and newborns given KI should see a doctor afterward if one can be found (FDA, pp. 9–10; EPA Q&A, p. 38).
  • Repeat daily only while the risk of breathing or eating radioactive iodine continues (FDA, p. 9). Once the cloud has passed, FDA says the protection is controlling the food — especially fresh milk — not more KI. Stored grain and canned milk or vegetables kept weeks to months carry no radioiodine risk (FDA, p. 11).
  • Too much is dangerous too — don't double up "to be safe" (CDC).

🥫 Food and water

(EPA Protective Action Q&A, p. 29; FDA; Nuclear War Survival Skills Ch. 8)

  • Food that was indoors is fine. Sealed cans, jars and bags that were outside are fine after wiping the outside — bag the wiping cloths. Stored grain and canned food have no radioactive-iodine risk.
  • Don't eat garden produce or anything left uncovered outdoors until levels have dropped and it's declared clear — then wash and peel it. Leafy vegetables: strip the outer leaves of cabbage and lettuce and wash the rest thoroughly. Roots and tubers: normal washing and peeling is enough. Fruit: wash before eating. (FB 2107, p. 14; PA-515, p. 7)
  • Don't drink fresh milk from animals grazing fallout-covered pasture for the first weeks (radioactive iodine concentrates in milk). Milk made into cheese and aged two months or more is fine — the radioactive iodine (Iodine-131) decays away (it loses half its strength every 8 days — USDA Ag Handbook 234, p. 9; USDA puts the wait at 30–60 days — PA-515, p. 8). If you have a baby, keep canned or dry milk in a protected place.
  • Water: bottled and sealed water is certain. Covered wells, tanks and cisterns are the best sources; deep wells are well protected. Tap water is fine for washing.
  • Boiling does not remove radioactivity. To clean doubtful water: stir in clay soil, let it settle several hours, and pour off the clear water — or filter it through a bucket of packed earth. Both remove most fallout particles. Then disinfect as usual.
  • Cover rain barrels, tanks and well heads ahead of time. (Rain barrel)

Water sources, safest first (NWSS 1979, pp. 76–77): deep wells and covered tanks → covered shallow wells → deep lakes → shallow ponds → streams (worst after the first heavy rains) → roof runoff → melted snow. With the power off, a well can still be used with a homemade bail-can on a rope (bail-can drawing; Well).

Settling method (NWSS 1979, pp. 78–79):

  1. Fill a bucket three-quarters full of the doubtful water.
  2. Dig clay or clayey soil from at least 4 inches below the surface (fallout stays near the top).
  3. Stir in about 1 inch of dry clay for every 4 inches of water until it's all suspended.
  4. Let it settle at least 6 hours. The clay carries the fallout down.
  5. Dip or siphon off the clear water, then disinfect it.

(USDA says settle a day, then strain through cloth; for stock, stir up a pond's mud bottom and let it settle — PA-515, p. 8.)

Earth filter (NWSS 1979, pp. 77–78; cut-away drawing of the filter can):

  1. Punch about a dozen nail holes, from the bottom upward, within 2 inches of the center of the bottom of a 5-gallon can or bucket.
  2. Put in 1½ inches of washed pebbles, then one layer of towel or burlap cut a bit bigger than the can.
  3. Add 6–7 inches of crumbled soil with some clay in it, dug from 4+ inches deep, pressed down in layers. Not pure clay (too tight) or sand (too loose).
  4. Cover with one layer of towel held by a few stones. Set the can on sticks over a pan.
  5. Pour in (settled) water. Expect about 6 quarts an hour at first, 2 quarts an hour later. If it runs faster than a quart in 10 minutes, repack the soil. Replace the soil after about 50 quarts. Disinfect the filtered water.

Disinfecting afterward: boil, or use plain unscented bleach — 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon, double if cloudy or very cold, wait 30 minutes (EPA Emergency Disinfection, pp. 1–2).

🐄 Livestock and gardens

(USDA FB 2107, pp. 8–14, PA-516; Oregon ODOE, p. 2)

  • Shelter animals if you can (a barn helps), and take them off pasture. Feed protected feed — hay from inside the stack, covered silage, grain from closed bins (USDA plan: dairy barn with a built-in family fallout shelter; Silage).
  • Water from a covered well, tank or flowing spring is best; open pond water clears more slowly but improves quickly.
  • Hold off on slaughter, fresh milk and eggs until levels drop.
  • Gardens: stop harvesting; after levels drop, wash and peel produce. Fallout on soil decays and is diluted by digging it in.

More from USDA FB 2107 (pp. 8–14):

  • Animals are as sensitive to radiation as people. Crowd them into the barn — they shield each other; ventilation, not floor space, is the limit. Dairy animals first (Dairy cattle) — milk them before fallout arrives if you can, and turn calves in with the cows (pp. 9–11).
  • Feed: fallout only coats the outside of a haystack or stack of feed bags. Peel off the outer layer and feed the inside; a tarp over a field stack ahead of time is enough (p. 10).
  • Tanks and ponds: use what's already in a covered tank first. Pond water usually clears in a few days — keep stock off it a few days longer if it rains (pp. 9–10).
  • Poultry stand about twice the radiation cattle do; eggs clear soon after hens get clean feed (PA-515, p. 8).
  • Meat: an animal exposed only to outside radiation can be eaten if slaughtered before it shows sickness; keep hide and gut contents off the meat. Wash fallout off animals with any water, wearing coveralls, gloves and boots (p. 12).
  • Pasture: lightly contaminated pasture can be grazed again after one to a few weeks (p. 13). Bees: don't move or harvest hives until checked (ODOE, p. 2).

Long-term garden choices: radioactive strontium (Strontium-90) acts like calcium. Liming acid soil cuts how much plants take up. Alfalfa, clover, soybeans and leafy greens take up more than grains, grasses, corn, potatoes and fruit — favor the latter on contaminated ground (FB 2107, p. 13).

📟 Measuring radiation

  • A meter turns guessing into decisions — when to leave the shelter, which room is safest, whether the water is clear.
  • KFM (Kearny Fallout Meter): a homemade meter from a can, foil and paper. Build it before you need it. The patterns must print exactly to scale — check against a ruler; OISM warns online copies of the templates aren't fully reliable and recommends the printed book. Full patterns and tables: ORNL-5040 and Nuclear War Survival Skills Appendix C. See it first: photos of a finished KFM, full-scale cut-away drawing. It is a kind of electroscope.
  • Old civil-defense meters turn up at sales: the yellow CD V-715 reads high levels (fallout); the CD V-700 is a low-range Geiger counter for checking food, water and people (Geiger counter). Manuals and a home-study course are in Nuclear-Radiation\Measuring-Radiation.

What a KFM is made of (ORNL-5040, pp. 33, 39): an 8-ounce can (or a cut-down soup can), 2 square feet of aluminum foil, clean thread, clear plastic, doorbell wire. A drying agent goes in the bottom — gypsum from heated drywall, or silica gel, not calcium chloride or salt. Charge it by rubbing a plastic ruler on dry paper (the other way: charging with unrolled tape).

Using it (ORNL-5040, pp. 69–73):

  1. Charge it and read the gap between the two foil leaves on the paper scale.
  2. Hold it on a stick about 3 feet above the ground for a set time — 1 minute if you have no idea of the level — then read again. The table on the meter turns the drop into a dose rate.
  3. Dose = dose rate × time. At 1,200 R/hr, a 30-second dash outside is 10 R.
  4. Keep a daily dose record for every person, and use the meter indoors to find the lowest-dose spot in your shelter, then add shielding there.

Civil-defense instruments (DCPA course):

Instrument Range Use
CD V-715 (yellow) 0–500 R/hr Fallout levels outside and in the shelter (picture)
CD V-717 0–500 R/hr, 25-ft remote probe Read outside levels without leaving shelter (p. 109; picture)
CD V-700 0–50 mR/hr Low levels: food, water, people, training (p. 89; drawing)
CD V-742 Pocket dosimeter, reads to 200 R Your total dose; recharge with a CD V-750 (p. 83; the scale you read; Dosimeter)

If this goes wrong:

Problem What to do
KFM won't hold a charge The air is too damp. Above about 90% humidity it can't be charged — make and use the dry-bucket (ORNL-5040, pp. 65, 73; dry-bucket drawing). Check the drying agent.
Leaves leak charge fast Modern thread and floss may not insulate. Use 2-lb monofilament fishing line or 1/16-inch strips of dry-cleaner bag (ORNL-5040 preface, p. 1).
Old CD V meter reads nothing Check batteries and run its operational check before trusting it (DCPA, p. 116).

🩺 If this goes wrong in the shelter

Problem What to do
Shelter is getting too hot Move air (a KAP or fan from outside air), strip to minimal clothes, drink freely and add salt to food (NWSS 1979, p. 62; only one opening? using a "divider" with a KAP).
Someone is vomiting Nausea and vomiting are the first signs of radiation sickness (H-20, pp. 10–11) — but stress and bad food cause them too. It isn't contagious. Keep them drinking small sips of the rehydration drink (Field care). (NWSS's "slightly salted water", about a teaspoon of salt per quart, is saltier than needed, especially for children — NWSS 1979, p. 114.) See the Medical guides.
Water is running low Drink as needed anyway. Plan a short trip when levels allow; use the settling and earth-filter methods above. Water stored soon after fallout gets safer with time as iodine decays (NWSS 1979, p. 79).
Someone must go out for medicine Only if life depends on it — EPA calls it a last resort after asking neighbors. Strip and wash on return (EPA Q&A, p. 43).
A nursing mother was outside If she has stored breast milk or formula, use it for now; if not, keep breastfeeding — feeding the baby comes first (EPA Q&A, p. 36).
The radio is silent Listen on a schedule to save batteries and try every band (rule of thumb). See the Radio guide.

📍 Our area

  • The region's only operating nuclear power plant is the Columbia Generating Station at Hanford, Washington — roughly 350 miles from the Illinois Valley, far outside its planning zones. (Oregon ODOE, p. 1; Columbia Generating Station, Hanford Site) Its zones are a 10-mile plume zone (Oregon is not in it) and a 50-mile ingestion zone that reaches parts of northern Oregon (ODOE, p. 2).
  • The more realistic threats here are fallout drifting from detonations elsewhere and fallout carried across the Pacific on west winds. After Chernobyl (Chernobyl disaster), radioactive iodine showed up in milk in northwest Oregon. (Nuclear War Survival Skills, 1987) Even in the 1960s, Chinese test fallout put measurable radioactive iodine in American milk (NWSS 1979, pp. 117–118). Winds across the U.S. run mostly west to east, but change day to day (FEMA H-20, p. 7).
  • OISM, publisher of Nuclear War Survival Skills, is in Cave Junction — the printed edition (with accurate KFM patterns) may be easy to get locally (Nuclear War Survival Skills).
  • Stay tuned locally: NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts on seven frequencies, 162.400–162.550 MHz (FEMA Planning Guidance, p. 167; NOAA Weather Radio). Which stations reach the valley, and local AM/FM, are on the Illinois Valley page.
