🔥 Wildfire: Defending Your Place With No Grid

How a household in the Illinois Valley can get its people, house, animals and land through a wildfire when no engine, air tanker, alert or phone is coming: reading the danger, hardening and clearing with hand tools, water without the grid, hand line, deciding ahead whether to stay or go, the fire's arrival, and the years after. The basics (5-ft and 30-ft rules, vent mesh, evacuation levels, a cleaner-air room) are in the Illinois Valley guide ("Wildfire"), Home Defense ("Fire as a threat to your home") and the Shelter guide ("Earthquake and wildfire hardening"). Most books are in Southern-Oregon\Wildfire: the federal firefighters' Incident Response Pocket Guide (IRPG), fire-behavior guide (PMS 437) and tactics guide (PMS 465); the Australian Country Fire Authority's (CFA) plan for staying to defend a house, adapted here because it assumes phone apps and daily ratings; Cohen's Forest Service research on how homes ignite; the State Fire Marshal (OSFM); and OSU Extension. FEMA P-737 is in Southern-Oregon\Shelter-Building\Wildfire-Quake-Hardening, Fire Weather (AH 360) in Southern-Oregon\Navigation-Weather\Weather, and the thinning and slash bulletins in Southern-Oregon\Firewood\Woodlot.

The short version

  • Leaving early beats staying late. Deaths "can occur because people wait too long to leave" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 2); leaving early "does not mean waiting to see or smell smoke" (CFA, p. 11). Decide before fire season, not on the day.
  • Embers burn most houses: up to 90% of ignitions (OSFM, p. 5), from fires 1 km (0.6 mile) or more away (Cohen, p. 4). Flames must be close: big flame fronts don't light wood walls beyond 40 m (130 ft) (p. 2). Keep 5 ft bare, 30 ft lean, 100 ft thinned.
  • Never stay to defend without at least two fit adults, about 2,600 gallons of water, pumps that work with the power out and protective clothing (CFA, p. 3). Children, elders and the sick leave early.
  • An east wind can blow 24–48 hours, strongest at night and early morning, with humidity "extremely low around the clock" (PMS 465, p. 227). Don't count on the fire "lying down" at night.
  • Hand tools only stop flames under 4 ft (IRPG, p. 66). Never fight the head of a fire; work from the burned side, with two ways out.
  • As the front passes, shelter inside: "You will be unable to survive out in the open" (CFA, p. 9). Then patrol for embers: "Many structures do not burn until after the fire front has passed" (IRPG, p. 31).
  • After the fire, burned trees fall, stump holes burn feet, and ½ inch of rain an hour on a burned slope can start a debris flow (USGS, p. 1).
  • No open burning June–October, until real rain (rule of thumb). Burn piles only on wet, calm days.

🌡️ Reading fire danger with no forecast

With no Red Flag Warnings, add wind, fuel dryness and smoke to the daily log in the Navigation & Weather guide (rule of thumb). The region's fire season "usually runs from June through September" (AH 360, p. 211), but "September bears the greatest impact of easterly winds" (PMS 465, p. 227); the 2020 fires burned in September.

Watch Danger rises when
Humidity Lowest about the hottest hour, rising slowly overnight (AH 360, p. 50). At 15–30%: "Very high ignition, rapid buildup, extensive spotting and crowning"; under 15%: "critical fire conditions" (IRPG, p. 65).
How dry it feels "snap of sticks, crackle of litter, pop vs crumble of leaves, dust in duff"; cured grass, snags, slash, crowns closer than 20 ft (IRPG, p. 52).
Wind Over 10 mph (IRPG, p. 50). No gauge: 8–12 mph, small twigs in constant motion; 13–18, dust raised on the road; 19–24, "Branchlets are broken from trees" (p. 59; Beaufort scale).
Unstable air Gusty wind, dust devils, very clear air, smoke rising straight up; steady wind and low haze mean stable air (AH 360, p. 71; IRPG, p. 50).
Time of day The "critical burn period" is 2 to 5 p.m. (IRPG, p. 19). At night slope winds turn downhill at 2–5 mph (PMS 437, p. 37), but fires in the mid-slope thermal belt stay active (Navigation & Weather guide; IRPG, p. 51).
Slope and aspect Fire runs uphill and up-canyon; saddles "are one of the most hazardous locations for homes" (FEMA P-737, pp. 17–18). Southwest slopes have the driest afternoon fuels (AH 360, p. 199). Over 20% slope (20 ft rise in 100 ft) or 10 mph wind, safety zones may need "more than double" (IRPG, p. 21); the saying that fire doubles its speed for every 10° of slope has no book behind it (rule of thumb).

East wind: the night is not safe

"East wind in fire control means an exceptionally dry wind from an easterly quadrant that may blow continuously for 24 to 48 hours or longer. East wind often reaches maximum strength during night and early morning hours. Surface wind of 60 mph is not uncommon", with humidity "extremely low around the clock" (PMS 465, p. 227; Foehn wind). When fire turns wind-driven, "This change can occur quickly" (p. 262). The 2020 numbers and warning signs are in Navigation & Weather ("Illinois Valley weather through the year"). On an east-wind day (rule of thumb): a lookout awake all night; the car fueled, packed and facing out; tanks full; protective clothes laid out; no saw, mower, grinder or fire of any kind; a leaving time agreed with the neighbors.

Smoke columns, spotting and flame length

  • The column: smoke trapped under an inversion means calmer fire; when it "begins to boil through the inversion ... Expect lowering RH, possible wind shifts, and increasing flames, spread, and spotting" (IRPG, p. 54). "sudden calm, smoke at your feet" mean a wind change; torching trees and more spot fires mean a blow-up (p. 53).
  • Spotting (embers starting fires ahead) is usually 100 ft to ¼ mile, sometimes miles; the Cerro Grande Fire spotted 10 miles ahead (PMS 437, p. 153). A home "within one mile of a natural area" is in the ember zone (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 3): every home here.
  • Where people die: "On relatively small fires or deceptively quiet areas of large fires", in light fuels "such as grass", in a wind shift, when fire runs uphill, from 2 to 5 p.m.; "Blowup to burnover ... can be as little as 5 minutes" (IRPG, p. 19).
Flame length Fire What hand tools can do (IRPG, p. 66)
Under 1 ft Smoldering "Handline holds"
1–4 ft Creeping, 2–5 chains an hour Attack the head or flanks; a hand line "should hold"
4–8 ft Running, 6–20 chains an hour "Too intense for direct attack with handtools"
8–11 ft Torching and spotting Work at the head is "ineffective"
Over 11 ft Crown fire to extreme Over 25 ft: "Escape to safety should be considered"

A chain is 66 ft. The county expects flames under 8 ft on the Rogue and Illinois valley floors and over 11 ft in the southern mountains (County plan, p. 71), so a household with hand tools works the flanks and the slow back edge, never the head (rule of thumb).

🏠 How houses catch fire

Three ways: embers land on something that burns; a surface fire creeps to the walls or roof; or radiant heat from burning plants or buildings nearby. "Most commonly, homes are lost in wildfires when they come into contact with embers or low-intensity surface fires — not from a giant wall of flames" (OSU EM 9247, p. 2).

  • Embers cause up to 90% of house fires (OSFM, p. 5), get in "through vents, windows, and other openings" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 3), and pile up where snow drifts on the roof and deck (p. 5).
  • Flames have to be close. By Cohen's calculations even crown fires won't light wood walls more than 40 m (130 ft) away (Cohen, p. 2): there a wall needs over 10 minutes of heat, but crown-fire flames last about 1 minute in one spot (p. 3). Test walls 10 m (33 ft) from crown fires didn't ignite, and homes with nonflammable roofs and 10–18 m (33–60 ft) of clearance survived 95% of the time (pp. 3–4).
  • The house can be the fuel: homes with flammable roofs "ignite and burn without the surrounding vegetation also burning" (Cohen, p. 4). The roof "is the most vulnerable component" (EM 9247, p. 3).
  • Late burning comes from "residual spot fires or creeping ground fire" (IRPG, p. 31). Single-pane glass can fail within 5 minutes (P-737, p. 47); glazing: Home Defense ("Stronger windows and glass doors").

The zones around the house

From the house Goal What to do
0–5 ft Nothing that burns Gravel, rock, pavers or bare soil; no plants, bark or wood chips; nothing against the walls (OSFM, p. 7; PNW 590, p. 7; EC 1574, p. 4)
5–30 ft Lean, clean and green Tree tops 15–20 ft apart; prune 6–10 ft up, keeping half the crown; no woodpile (EM 9247, pp. 4–5; OSFM, p. 13)
30–100 ft (200 on steep slopes) Fire stays on the ground Grass under 4 in, 10 ft between crowns, no ladder fuels, no snags (OSFM, pp. 14–16; EM 9184, p. 11)
Beyond 100 ft A slower, cooler fire Thin over the years; a few snags per acre for wildlife (EM 9184, pp. 11–12)

The books number their zones differently, so this table uses feet (the zones). Clearing need "only occur within a few tens of meters from a home" (Cohen, p. 4): finish the inner zones first (rule of thumb).

🔨 Hardening the house with what you have

Vents, gutters and woodpiles are in the Shelter guide; windows, fences, gates and the driveway in Home Defense. The order:

  1. Roof first. Clean it and the gutters spring and fall, and in summer under madrones, which drop leaves then (EM 9184, p. 9); debris gathers in valleys and where roof meets wall (P-737, p. 25). Untreated wood shakes are unrated and "not desirable" (BCD, p. 3): keep hand-split shakes for sheds, and salvage metal roofing from buildings you won't defend (rule of thumb).
  2. Vents: 1/8-inch metal mesh over the old 1/4–1/2 in screen, which "may be plastic" (OSFM, p. 8). This follows current Oregon advice over FEMA's 2008 "maximum opening of 1/4 inch" (P-737, p. 40). 1/16 in clogs, and clogged debris can make embers; keep cardboard away from vents (BCD, p. 5).
  3. Vent covers. Ready, Set, Go! says cover attic and ground vents with "pre-cut plywood" (p. 9); FEMA advises against it because people fall (P-737, p. 42). So cut and label covers now; always fit the ground and crawlspace vents; gable vents only from a tied-off ladder with time to spare (rule of thumb).
  4. Gaps and gutters. Caulk gaps; open eaves trap heat (BCD, p. 6). Seal pipe and wire holes with mortar (P-737, p. 72; lime mortar: Masonry & Roads). Vinyl gutters "can fail under radiant heat" (EM 9247, p. 3): use metal.
  5. Windows and walls. "Metal blinds are best to protect the interior from radiant heat" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 6). Rotting siding lets embers catch (OSFM, p. 9); siding down to the ground lets fire into the wall (BCD, p. 4). Nothing stored under an open foundation, and no lattice (P-737, pp. 57–58).
  6. Decks. Clear on, around and under; 1/8-in metal mesh under deck openings (OSFM, p. 10). Furniture and "even welcome mats" catch embers (EM 9247, p. 4).
  7. Woodpile and lumber 30 ft away (OSFM, p. 13; ODF's handout says 20, every other source 30), level with or uphill of the house: "Embers that settle into your wood pile will slowly build a fire" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 7).
  8. Propane and fuel. Clear 10 ft around a fixed tank, which will "vent when overheated" and isn't "typically at risk of exploding"; BBQ cylinders 30 ft away (OSFM, p. 12). Fuel, including the fire pump's gas, 30 ft from the house and "away from downhill slopes" (P-737, p. 73).
  9. Outbuildings 50 ft from the house (P-737, p. 23); "Wet hay can start fires through spontaneous combustion" (4-H Horse, p. 61). Well power supplies "are vulnerable during a wildfire" (P-737, p. 72): clear around the pump house, panels and tank (rule of thumb).

🪓 Clearing around the house with hand tools

Work from the house outward (rule of thumb). The books differ on trees; this guide uses:

  • Spacing: at least 10 ft between crowns within 100 ft (OSFM, p. 14), 15–20 ft within 30 ft of the house and more on slopes (EM 9247, p. 5); branches 15 ft from the house (PNW 590, p. 7), 10 ft from a chimney (OSFM, p. 15).
  • Limbing: 6 ft up, or a third of the height on trees under 18 ft (OSFM, p. 14); mature trees 10 ft, taking no more than a third of the live branches at once; conifers in fall and winter, hardwoods in spring (EM 9184, p. 7).
  • Keep some: deciduous trees "block embers" (OSFM, p. 15); mature ponderosa pine and bigleaf maple "catch and filter embers" (EM 9247, p. 5).

Brush and ladder fuels. Small trees and brush under big ones are "ladder" fuels that carry a ground fire into the crowns (EC 1573, p. 2). Space shrubs 2–3 times their height apart (EM 9184, p. 16), none over one metre (about 3 ft) by a window (CFA, p. 4). Worst burners: Scotch broom, gorse, blackberry, juniper, many manzanitas and ceanothus (EM 9184, p. 15).

  • Oak, madrone and tanoak resprout, and the sprouts "can quickly become new ladder fuels"; sometimes don't cut (EM 9184, pp. 15–17). Black oaks cut December–May sprouted 92%, June–November 45% (PSW-GTR-185, p. 36), but summer tool work starts fires: cut in late fall, after the first real rain (rule of thumb).
  • Blackberry: dig the crowns with a Pulaski or grub hoe (EM 9184, pp. 20–21). Scotch broom: cutting old plants in late summer may kill them, but don't graze it: it is "toxic to livestock in large quantities" (p. 20).

Grass under 4 in within 100 ft (OSFM, p. 16). Dry grass makes flames "three times the height of the grass": cut it green, before fire season; in season mow only early in the morning (EM 9184, p. 9).

Slash with no chipper. Lop and scatter it 12–18 in deep at most; it only moves the fuel, dry slash takes "a decade or more" to rot, and never "within 100 feet of the home" (EC 1574, p. 2). Hand-pile pieces under 6 in across (p. 3) and burn them in winter (see "Burning on purpose").

Animals mow too. Cattle keep grass down; goats and sheep browse brush but need fencing, water and predator control (EM 9184, p. 14). Keep it up: without upkeep the benefit is "lost" in 5 to 10 years (p. 3); work the 30–100 ft zone in sections, all of it every 5–8 years (p. 5). Brush the driveway back 10 ft: "If firefighters can't see down your driveway, they will not enter" (p. 7).

Which work in which season

Season Work Source

💧 Water with no power

"Loss of power will prevent ... electric pumps from working" (CFA, p. 8), so plan water that needs no grid. And "Never rely on water for firefighter safety" (IRPG, p. 31): water is not your escape plan.

What How much Source
To defend a house 10,000 litres (about 2,640 gal) or more, with gear that works if the power goes out CFA, p. 3
Reserve you never touch 100 gal IRPG, p. 31
Filling 100 ft of hose ¾-in 2 gal; 1-in 4 gal IRPG, p. 104
Forestry nozzle, 3/16-in tip 7 gal a minute at 50 psi IRPG, p. 104
A fire hose 50–125 gal a minute P-737, p. 67
Water extinguisher 2.5 gal, reaching 30–40 ft CERT, p. 170

At 7 gal a minute, 2,640 gallons lasts about 6 hours; a fire hose empties it in 20–50 minutes (arithmetic). Use small nozzles, short bursts and dirt, and save water for embers.

Storing it

  • Tanks of "steel, concrete, or ferrocement"; fiberglass "may be placed underground" (P-737, p. 77). Clear 10 ft around a plastic tank and bury its pipe (rule of thumb). A tank up the hill: Storing Energy ("Gravity that works"); each 2 ft of height gives about 1 psi (IRPG, p. 104).
  • Ponds, pools and creeks. A "dry hydrant" (fixed suction pipe) needs water at least 2 ft deep (P-737, p. 78). Oregon needs no water right for "emergency firefighting" (OWRD, p. 2). Keep a hard road within 10 ft (PNW 783, p. 7). Inside, fill the bath and buckets (CFA, p. 7).

Getting it there

  • A gas fire pump lifts at most 22 ft at sea level, minus 1 ft per 1,000 ft of elevation (IRPG, p. 104): about 20 ft on the valley floor (arithmetic). Pumping uphill, fit a check-and-bleeder valve (same page). Keep "sufficient fuel which is clean" (CFA, p. 6); run it each spring and time how long a gallon lasts (rule of thumb). The well: Water Wells ("Running the well pump without the grid").
  • Backpack pumps, buckets, mops. "A few squirts from a backpack pump can easily cool down hot embers" (PMS 465, p. 85); most hold about 5 gal (rule of thumb). CFA adds "mops, metal buckets, ladders and rakes" (p. 5).
  • Hoses to reach every side of the house (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 7), but "Long hose lays are not recommended"; "Charge garden hoses" (fill them) beforehand (IRPG, p. 31). Plastic melts: bring plastic fittings and hoses in before the front (CFA, p. 9); use brass (rule of thumb).
  • Sprinklers on the ridge or under the eaves, fed by a tank (P-737, pp. 68–69), though "High winds ... can significantly degrade" them (p. 70). Ready, Set, Go!'s "Don't leave sprinklers on" (p. 9) is about town pressure; with a tank, turn them on "When embers start to appear", and "Block downpipes and fill gutters with water" (CFA, p. 8).
  • Dirt is water too. "Water can be used very sparingly but effectively when hand tools are used to expose burning fuels and mix soil with fire" (PMS 465, p. 85). "Let no person use water alone, but always with another person with a hand tool" (p. 52); "If a pump should fail, enough hand tools should be available to extinguish the fire with mineral soil" (p. 91).

⛏️ Hand tools and firebreaks

Pulaski: ax plus grub hoe, "about 5½ pounds"; short, shallow swings, the hoe landing on its corner; goggles; "Never sharpen the top of the hoe" (MTDC, p. 25). McLeod: rake plus hoe, "inefficient in rocky or unusually brushy areas" (p. 26). Fire shovel: scrapes "down to mineral soil" (p. 29). Carry tools "at your side and downhill, with blades forward"; "Never throw a tool" (p. 11). Saws in fire season: Firewood guide ("Bucking and limbing").

Building a hand line

A hand line (fireline) is a strip cleared of fuel and scraped to bare mineral soil (Firebreak).

  1. Anchor it to something that won't burn: road, creek, rock, plowed field or burned ground (IRPG, p. 23). Building "without safe anchor point" is one of the 18 Watch Out Situations (p. 140).
  2. Width: "one and one half times as wide as the dominate fuel is high", 1–3 ft of it scraped to mineral soil, on the side away from the fire; in timber, 20–30 ft cleared with a 3–4 ft scrape (PMS 465, p. 42). Brush 6 ft tall needs a 9-ft line (arithmetic).
  3. Down to mineral soil, or fire creeps through roots; unburned material goes outside the line, burning material into the burn (p. 37). Limb the trees on both sides: heat crosses a narrow line without flames touching (pp. 40–41).
  4. Below a fire on a slope, dig a "cup trench" to catch rolling logs and cones (p. 38); above it, the steeper the slope, the wider the line (p. 17).
  5. Short and straight, tied to roads, rock and creeks; on a ridge, on top or just over (IRPG, p. 101). Never build line downhill toward a fire, mid-slope above it, or with unburned fuel between you and it (p. 20).

The burning edge itself is the "Safest place to work; firefighters can usually escape into the burned area" (IRPG, p. 99). "Most firelines will be unsuccessful in stopping a crown fire" (PMS 465, p. 42).

How fast can you build line?

Fuel 20-person crew, chains an hour (SDTDC, p. 10) Per person, feet an hour (arithmetic)
Short grass 9.5 about 30
Chaparral, brush, timber litter 6.8–7 about 22

Those are trained crews, measured over a whole shift. The older PMS 465 gives 1–2 chains (66–132 ft) per person-hour in our forests (p. 228); plan on the measured rates. Three adults might build 70 ft an hour in brush (arithmetic), while fire there spreads 2–10 chains (130–660 ft) an hour (same page). You can't out-dig a fire on the day: build breaks in the wet season, and on the day use tools on spot fires, the 5-ft strip and the burned edge (rule of thumb).

Small fires, spot fires and wet sacks

  • "Don't overestimate your ability" (ODFW, p. 34). "Do not ever try to fight a fire alone"; "Always have two ways to exit"; "If you feel the heat, you are too close" (CERT, p. 173).
  • Dirt first: throw mineral soil, scrape a line around, stir hot embers into dirt (PMS 465, pp. 52, 91).
  • In grass: let it burn to a barrier, "cold-trail" the dead edge (feel along it, line any live spot), or use a "gunnysack or swatter" (PMS 465, pp. 30, 59; Fire flapper). Soak a burlap sack, work from the burned side, press or drag it on the base of the flames instead of flapping, and re-wet it often (rule of thumb).
  • "Getting frequent spot fires across line" is a Watch Out: time to go (IRPG, p. 140).

Burning out: mostly don't

Burning out is "setting fire inside the fireline to consume fuel between the fireline and the edge of the fire", so crews "keep one foot in the black"; backfiring sets fire well ahead of the main fire, a riskier method (PMS 465, pp. 112–113). Firing needs lookouts, escape routes, anchor points and "Adequate resources ... for both firing and holding" (IRPG, p. 26). Household rule (rule of thumb): never backfire. Burn out only a narrow strip of grass or litter off a finished, anchored line, with the wind carrying it away from the line, someone holding the line with water and tools, and a lookout. If unsure, don't.

🚪 Stay or go: decide before fire season

"Do not wait to be advised to leave" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 10). Alerts failed in 2020 (What to Expect guide); now there are none. Stay only if every answer is yes (CFA, p. 3):

Places you can't defend: "narrow canyon bottoms, mid-slope with fire below, and narrow ridges near chimneys and saddles" (IRPG, p. 28). Worst-weather days: "Do not plan to defend on CATASTROPHIC days" (CFA, p. 3). With no ratings, call it catastrophic when an east wind breaks twigs from trees and humidity is under 15%, or leaves crumble to dust (rule of thumb, from CFA, p. 2 and IRPG, pp. 59–65); then leave "The night before" or "Early in the morning" (CFA, p. 11).

Triggers: write them down

A trigger is something you can see that means "go now". Leaving early "does not mean waiting for a warning or a siren" (CFA, p. 11); firefighters "Set trigger point for safe retreat" (IRPG, p. 30). Examples (rule of thumb): a worst-weather day; a smoke column building over a named ridge upwind; ash or embers falling; fire crossing a named road or creek (time your escape against its spread, IRPG, p. 21); any one adult saying "go".

LCES for a household

Safety zones: how big

"Separation distance between the firefighter and the flames should be at least four times the maximum continuous flame height" (IRPG, p. 22):

Flame height Distance to the flames Area
10 ft 40 ft 1/10 acre
20 ft 80 ft ½ acre
50 ft 200 ft 3 acres
100 ft 400 ft 12 acres

That counts radiant heat only, on flat ground with no wind, for a three-person crew, and is "Not intended for structure protection" (same page). With wind over 10 mph or slope over 20%, it may need "more than double" (p. 21).

Getting out and last resort

  • Keep "old shoes and a flashlight handy in case of a sudden evacuation at night" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 8). Lock up (CFA, p. 11); open any wood gate joined to the house to break the "wick" (OSFM, p. 11). Don't leave "the key in the ignition" (FEMA 2004, p. 127): keep it on a hook by the door (rule of thumb).
  • Caught outside (IRPG, p. 46): "Escape if you can." In light fuels "you may be able to move through the flames into the black"; on a flank, "try to get below the fire"; "Use bodies of water" and "heat barriers such as large rocks"; "Structures and vehicles may be an option for temporary refuge". Expect "Extremely heavy ember showers" and a "Superheated air blast" before the flames; lie face down, protect your airway, and "WHEN IN DOUBT, WAIT IT OUT" (p. 47). You have no fire shelter: a pond or plowed ground is your last refuge (rule of thumb).

🔥 When the fire comes

Hours before the front

  1. Protective clothing on: "always first thing to do" (CFA, p. 8). Anyone not defending leaves now.
  2. Fill the bath, buckets and tanks; roll out hoses; set up and test pumps and backpack sprayers (CFA, p. 7).
  3. Clear the walls: furniture, doormats and BBQ cylinders away; inside, furniture away from windows (same page); cloth curtains down unless the blinds are metal (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 9).
  4. Close up: windows, doors, vent covers; gas off at the tank (same page); fans off so they can't draw embers in (P-737, pp. 42–43); wet wool blankets or cotton towels around the inside of windows and doors (CFA, p. 8); a ladder under the attic hatch and a flashlight in the attic (p. 7).
  5. Wet the house: fill the gutters, "Hose down the side of the house facing the fire", sprinklers on when embers appear; "Patrol for embers ... You may need to do this for several hours"; "Drink lots of cool water" (CFA, p. 8).

As the front passes: shelter inside

"As the fire front approaches it will become extremely hot outside. You will be unable to survive out in the open" (CFA, p. 9). Bring plastic hoses and fittings in, and go inside when "The skin on your ears and hands will alert you" (same page). Then:

  • Stay where you can see the fire. "Most bathrooms are unsuitable to shelter in": one door. Every shelter room needs two ways out; a window counts. Check the roof space for embers; drink water.
  • If the house catches fire: shut the door on the burning room, close doors as you move to the far end, then go out to burned ground. "Do not return to the house for any reason if it's on fire."

"If it's hot inside the house, it is four to five times hotter outside" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 9). The flames pass quickly: about a minute in one spot for a crown fire (Cohen, p. 3).

Right after: ember patrol

"Exercise extreme caution before deciding to go outside", then "Actively patrol your property for embers for hours after the fire has passed" (CFA, p. 10): roof lines and gutters, garden beds and mulch, outdoor furniture, woodpiles, doormats, decks, sheds, window ledges and door sills, under the house, inside the roof and under the floorboards (same page), and the attic (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 9). "Water down the outside of the house, including the roof" (CFA, p. 10); stay clear of "the structural collapse zone" (IRPG, p. 31). Duff, stumps and log piles smolder for days: patrol day and night for at least 3 days, then daily for 2 weeks and after every dry, windy day (rule of thumb).

😷 Smoke, heat and people

Protective clothing (CFA, p. 7): a wide-brimmed hat, smoke goggles, a P2 (N95-type) mask, a long-sleeved collared shirt and long pants of cotton or another natural fibre, "Tough leather garden gloves – not rubber or synthetic", sturdy boots, wool or cotton socks. Many synthetics "can melt or catch fire" (AFH 10-644, p. 177). If your clothes catch fire, "do not run ... Lie down and roll" (Scout Handbook 1911, p. 284).

A bandana is not a mask. "Cloth face coverings, bandanas (damp or dry), handkerchiefs ... should not be relied on" (EPA Smoke Guide, p. 78). CFA lists a cotton scarf as an option (p. 7); this guide follows the EPA: cotton shields skin from embers, not lungs. Use an N95 or P100 marked "NIOSH", two head straps, no ear loops; a beard breaks the seal; replace it when breathing gets hard; it raises heat-illness risk (EPA Protect Your Lungs, pp. 1–2). For ages 2 to 7 an N95 rarely fits (EPA Smoke Guide, p. 77): children leave early.

Smoke and carbon monoxide bring "headaches and visual impairment, then impaired decision-making, and possible death"; rotate people out, and don't sleep where "smoke can concentrate at night" (IRPG, p. 44). With no power for a filter fan, open windows at night to cool off even if some smoke comes in, then close them and the curtains by day (EPA Smoke Guide, p. 49); night inversions can hold smoke here, so look first.

Heat and burns. Drink "lots of cool water often even if you don't feel thirsty" (CFA, p. 9). Care: Field Care ("Heat and cold in the Valley"; "Burns"); exhaustion: Home Defense ("Stress, fatigue and judgment").

🐐 Animals and livestock

Leave early, in daylight, to places agreed ahead (PNW 783, pp. 7–8). "Never leave pets behind"; keep them "indoors or leashed" (ODA, p. 2). Horses: Horse guide ("Local dangers"); poultry and rabbits: Rabbits & Chickens.

If they must stay (PNW 783, p. 8): a pasture "light on fuel", such as one "heavily grazed", or a ploughed paddock (CFA, p. 4); "remove rugs and halters" (p. 7); "Leave gates open"; feed and water for "seven to 10 days"; irrigation on; electricity and propane off; "disked, plowed or scraped ground" around the barn; dry bedding wetted or removed.

A burning barn (4-H Horse, p. 61): "Never lock stall doors". Blindfold a horse that won't lead with a wet cloth. "Do not turn a horse loose, because it might run back into the barn." "Do not risk your own life".

After: burned animals may hide pain; badly burned ones may need to be put down (PNW 783, pp. 8–9). Burns "may worsen for up to six (6) weeks"; froth from nose or mouth means fluid in the lungs (ODA, pp. 1–2).

🌲 After the fire

Hot ground, stump holes and mop-up

  • Stump holes: roots burn underground under hot ash; "Minor burns are often experienced due to burning stump holes" (PMS 465, p. 262). Probe ash with a tool before stepping, and mark holes (rule of thumb).
  • Mop-up "is normally a slow, tedious job": "break up and scatter burning fuels, then cool them down with water. Dig, scrape, and scatter." Water alone "can actually insulate hot spots" (PMS 465, p. 85). "dig out burning roots" and stir embers into dirt (p. 52). Crews mop up a set distance, "100 feet, 500 feet" (p. 51); do at least 100 ft around buildings (rule of thumb).
  • Mix ash and dirt "until all steaming and sizzling stops"; "Never just bury a fire" (ODFW, p. 34). A smoldering log can "break out afresh with a rising wind" (Scout Handbook 1911, p. 186).

Burned trees

  • Warning signs: "Trees burning for any period of time"; "Dead, broken, or burning tops"; "Absence of needles, bark, or limbs"; "Leaning or hung-up trees"; "Potential for trees to domino" (IRPG, p. 36). Mark "No Work Zones" (p. 37).
  • Fall zone: a circle as wide as the tree is tall, up to 1.5 times, and farther downhill on a slope (FS-1273, p. 36). Trees with recent fire damage fail more (p. 56); near the house, take dead trees down the year they die (p. 84). Felling: Firewood guide ("Felling trees safely").

Ash, ruins and wells

  • Ash from burned buildings can hold arsenic, lead, asbestos and caustic chemicals; dust masks don't stop asbestos or carbon monoxide. A burned lithium battery that pops, hisses or smokes: move "upwind at least ... (330 feet)" (EPA Smoke Guide, p. 120).
  • Ash from CCA-treated decks and fences "may contain lethal amounts of arsenic". Mist, don't dry-sweep; wash garden produce; children, pregnant women and elders stay out of cleanup (p. 121).
  • Fence any new sinkhole, "as it may be a dug well" (OWRD, p. 1). Don't use a propane cylinder with "fire damage" (propane safety, p. 8).
  • Water: see Water Purification ("After a wildfire") and Water Wells ("Keeping well water clean").

Debris flows and erosion

A debris flow is a fast mudflow of soil, rock, trees and ash. On burned land one can start "after only a few minutes of heavy rainfall—about half an inch an hour or more", "days to years after a fire", travel many miles, and hit places "that were neither burned nor received any rain" (USGS, p. 1; Debris flow). USGS (same page): "LEAVE BEFORE A STORM ARRIVES. IF YOU WAIT, YOU ARE TOO LATE"; if you can't, "GET TO HIGH GROUND" (upstairs, the roof, uphill, a tree); listen for "cracking, breaking, roaring, or a fast-moving freight train". With no forecast, people below a burned slope go when the first hard rain of the season starts (rule of thumb).

Slowing erosion by hand. Severe heat can make the top few inches of soil shed water ("hydrophobic"); treat first where "streams, roads or buildings are directly downslope" (OSU EM 9562, p. 2).

  • Contour logs need no fuel: burned trees 6–10 in thick, 10–30 ft long, laid across the slope "in full contact" with the ground so soil settles behind them (EM 9562, p. 3).
  • Straw mulch at 1–2 tons an acre (p. 2); straw-bale check dams last only "the first few storm events" (p. 3). Don't sow non-native grass: it does little the first year and can "increase fire hazard as they cure" (p. 2).

Replanting

  • Grasses, shrubs and hardwoods return on their own. For conifers, "Most seed falls within one or two tree lengths" of a seed tree, and bare mineral soil is a good seedbed for most (OSU EM 9561, p. 1). Thick-barked black and Oregon white oaks resist heat; tanoak and canyon live oak "re-sprout after being top-killed" (KBO oak guide, p. 45).
  • Use seed from "parent trees adapted to local conditions"; plantings fail from poor site preparation, competing plants and animal damage (EM 9561, p. 3). With no nursery, gather cones and seedlings from surviving local trees (rule of thumb). Near the house, plant fire-resistant kinds (PNW 590, p. 7; OSU privacy plants).

🤝 Neighbors: lookouts, signals and shared breaks

"In rural areas, your neighbor can be at your home faster than any emergency response" (EM 9247, p. 2); now they are the only response. "Your Home Ignition Zone overlaps onto adjacent properties" (p. 5), and "your trees may pose a greater risk to your neighbor's home than to your own" (Ready, Set, Go!, p. 4).

  • Lookouts on worst-weather days, in turns, day and night, who know the triggers and sound the alarm "early, not late" (IRPG, p. 21; the rota (rule of thumb)).
  • Signals: agree an alarm ahead (Signals guide: "Whistles, bells and alarm codes", "The neighborhood OK/HELP plan"; Radio guide). Give a fire's "exact location" (ODFW, p. 34). No signal fires in fire season.
  • A meeting place such as a mailbox cluster, crossroads or central barn (MYN Rural, p. 1), and a road map of water, gates, fuel breaks, hazards and livestock (PNW 783, p. 7).
  • A road crew clears brush along the shared road out and keeps shared water signed and clear (P-737, p. 77). Mow or slash a 3 m (10 ft) break around each property (CFA, p. 4), joined along the lines; small owners can pool work (EC 1575, p. 3).
  • A new smoke is the best chance to stop a fire and also where firefighters die (IRPG, p. 19): go with tools and water, LCES first (rule of thumb). Organizing: Community & Barter ("Forming a neighborhood group"; "Keeping watch").

🍂 Burning on purpose: wet season only

The books put the fire season at June through September (AH 360, p. 211), but east winds peak in September and fall can stay dry. This library's rule: no open burning June–October, until real rain (rule of thumb). Oregon allowed burning only outside fire season, on burn days, with fines "up to $12,000 per day" (DEQ, p. 1), a permit from ODF and the fire district (EC 1574, p. 4), and liability "if the fire escapes" (p. 3). What not to burn, and the burn barrel: Sanitation ("Burning trash and brush, and ash").

Slash piles (EC 1574, pp. 3–4):

  1. In the open, 10 ft from trees, snags and brush, 100 ft from streams, away from buildings, propane and power lines.
  2. Twigs under ½ in at the bottom, limbs parallel, at least 4 ft wide and 4 ft high; cover it when about 80% built so it will light in the rain.
  3. Burn "during daylight, and when it's wet or rainy with little or no wind", with "a shovel and water nearby".
  4. Check it after: piles "may smolder for days or even weeks, roaring back to life in windy, warm weather, even during dry winter conditions" (p. 3). Stir and drown the last of it; look again after the next dry, windy day (rule of thumb).

"Many wildfires start from escaped burn piles", and broadcast burning around homes is "very risky" (EM 9184, p. 14). Never burn poison oak (p. 25).

Fire in the oak woods. The region's low-elevation oak woodlands are "fire adapted", kept by "Relatively frequent low- to mixed-severity fire"; prescribed burns there go "prior to leaf out in the spring or after leaf fall in the autumn" (KBO oak guide, p. 45). Cultural burning, the way the valley's first peoples tended the land with fire, is covered in "Tending the land with fire" in Skills of the Valley's First Peoples (Cultural burning).
