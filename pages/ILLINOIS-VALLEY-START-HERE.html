🔥 Wildfire

Evacuation levels (Josephine County)

Level Means Do
1 — BE READY (green) Danger in the area Pack, plan your route, move people with special needs, pets and livestock early.
2 — BE SET (yellow) Significant danger Be ready to leave at a moment's notice. Leaving now is fine. "This may be the only notice you receive."
3 — GO NOW (red) Leave immediately Don't stop to gather things or protect the house. "This will be the last notice you receive."

The state's advice: leave whenever you feel unsafe — you don't need to wait for a notice. Alerts come by Citizen Alert, Wireless Emergency Alerts on phones, radio/TV, door-to-door, and ham radio.

Go-kit (Oregon OEM checklist)

Medications and medical devices · glasses · food and plenty of water · important documents · phone chargers · battery AM/FM radio · first-aid kit · flashlight · N95 masks · long sleeves, long pants, sturdy shoes, hat. Plan two routes out. Move livestock early.

Around the house (defensible space)

  • First 5 ft around the house (the three zones): nothing that burns. Firewood and lumber 30 ft away (OSFM, p. 13; the ODF Josephine County handout says at least 20 ft).
  • Within 100 ft: grass mowed to 4 in or less, 10 ft between tree crowns, limbs 10 ft from the chimney, no "ladder fuels" (brush under trees — drawing).
  • Clean roofs and gutters; cover vents with 1/8-inch metal mesh (how embers get in); clear around propane tanks; keep the driveway clear enough for a fire engine.

Smoke

Make one cleaner-air room (windows shut, AC on recirculate). A DIY air cleaner = a box fan (made 2012 or later, UL/ETL label) + a MERV-13 furnace filter. Unplug it when you leave the house. Use N95 masks outdoors.

Details: the Wildfire\ folder (Ready-Set-Go guide, OSFM defensible-space guide, OSU fire-resistant plants, EPA/OHA smoke sheets). Fire news: swofire.com (ODF Southwest Oregon).

🌎 Earthquake (Cascadia) and tsunami

  • A Cascadia magnitude-9 quake would shake for 4–6 minutes, with aftershocks for months (CREW scenario, p. 10). Drop, Cover, Hold On.
  • Expect landslides on mountain roads and damage to bridges. Coast-to-inland routes like US-199 are exactly the kind expected to be cut, so the valley may be isolated for a long time.
  • State estimates for inland western Oregon: power out 1–3 months, water/sewer 1 month to 1 year, main highways 6–12 months to partly reopen, fuel shortages (Oregon's fuel hub is near Portland) (Oregon Resilience Plan, p. 6).
  • Plan to be on your own for at least 2 weeks — longer in rural areas.
  • At the coast (Brookings, Gold Beach, Crescent City): if you feel a long quake, walk to high ground immediately. The first waves can arrive in 15–30 minutes; surges continue 12+ hours and the first is not the largest.

Details: Earthquake-Tsunami\ (Living on Shaky Ground, 2 Weeks Ready toolkit, Cascadia scenario, tsunami evacuation maps for Brookings-Harbor, Gold Beach, Crescent City, Smith River).

☢️ Nuclear fallout

  • Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Fallout is dust; put mass between you and the roof and outside walls (drawing). It loses 90% of its strength in 7 hours and 99% in 2 days (FEMA H-20, p. 8).
  • A one-story wood house with no basement is poor shelter. Know now where you'd go: a basement or masonry building nearby, a shielded crawl space, or a trench shelter.
  • Potassium iodide (KI) protects only the thyroid, from radioactive iodine — adults 130 mg, children less (FDA, p. 9).
  • Everything else — shielding, how long to stay in, decontamination, food, water, livestock, homemade fallout meter: Nuclear Fallout Guide and Nuclear-Radiation\.

🦌 Hunting and 🎣 Fishing

While seasons and tags are still enforced, the 2026 rule booklets are in Hunting\ and Fishing\ (big game, fishing) (the 2027 big game and fishing booklets come out around December 2026 — see Keeping this current). With or without rules, the units, waters and seasons below tell you where game and fish are, and when — and leaving breeding animals and spawning runs alone is what keeps them there next year.

Hunting units: US-199 is the line. West of the highway (Illinois River canyon, Kalmiopsis) is Chetco Unit 27 (82% public land). East of it (Lake Selmac, Takilma, Oregon Caves, Williams) is Applegate Unit 28 (57% public land). In Cave Junction, Kerby, Selma and O'Brien, which side of the highway you're on decides the unit. North of the Rogue at Grants Pass is Evans Creek Unit 29.

2026 general seasons, for orientation (check the current booklet): western Oregon buck rifle Oct 3–Nov 6 · archery deer & elk Aug 29–Sep 27 · Unit 28 coast elk rifle Nov 14–17 and Nov 21–27 (Unit 27 is controlled hunts only) · black bear Aug 1–Dec 31 · cougar all year (quotas) — bear and cougar must be checked in at ODFW within 10 days · grouse and quail Sep 1–Jan 31 · fall turkey Sep 1–Jan 31 · spring turkey Apr 15–May 31. Chronic wasting disease has not been found in Oregon (it has in CA, ID and WA).

Fishing nearby (2026–27 rules):

  • Illinois River: artificial flies and lures only; wild trout must be released; all tributaries closed; closed Oct 1–Nov 30 from Eight Dollar Bridge to the forks; wild steelhead over 24 in may be kept only in listed sections and months.
  • Lake Selmac (Josephine County's largest lake): rainbow trout stocked Feb–May; largemouth bass (1 per day here), crappie, bluegill and bullhead in summer and fall. County park with boat ramps.
  • Rogue River: hatchery steelhead and coho open all year; Chinook seasons vary by section.
  • New for 2026: a Rogue-South Coast Steelhead Validation is required to fish for steelhead Dec 1–Apr 30.

Details, maps and fish ID: Hunting\ and Fishing\. Game care: Big Game from Hunt to Home (OSU), the NDSU Wild Side of the Menu series, canning, jerky and smoking guides. How-to: Hunting\How-To (hunter-ed handbook, shot placement, blood trailing, gutless quartering of deer and elk, bear skinning, plucking birds, track ID) and Fishing\How-To (knots, rigs, casting, cleaning and filleting).

Game meat safety:

  • Cook bear and other wild game to 165 °F inside. Freezing, curing, smoking or drying does not reliably kill the trichinella worms in wild game.
  • Wear gloves when cleaning rabbits and rodents (tularemia).
  • Shot with lead? Trim generously around the bullet path: fast-expanding lead bullets scatter fragments 5–14 in from the wound, sometimes 18 in (Minnesota DNR X-ray study, p. 6; the older 4-in rule in OSU PNW 517, p. 1 is too small). Give lead-shot meat to adults, not children or pregnant women (rule of thumb).
  • Don't bring deer or elk brain or spine parts back into Oregon from another state (chronic wasting disease rule).

Local game and its habits, field dressing in hot weather, butchering, snares and fish traps, and game diseases: Hunting, Trapping & Fishing for Food.

🪤 Trapping

How-to: start with the national Trapper Education Manual and the NY trapper manual (trap types, baits, water and land sets, snares, running a trapline, fur handling), then the species guides (beaver, coyote, raccoon, bobcat, gray fox). For improvised traps with no store-bought gear, see the 1907 Deadfalls and Snares (illustrated) and the snare and deadfall chapters of the Army survival manuals (Manuals\ArmyFieldManuals; FM 21-76, p. 95). Hides and furs: USDA Home Tanning of Leather and Small Fur Skins.

  • Check traps at least daily — animals spoil, suffer, or get taken by other predators.
  • Beaver, raccoon, muskrat and rabbits are the most dependable meat from traps here; wear gloves for rabbits and rodents (tularemia), and cook all wild meat thoroughly.
  • Don't catch your own dogs: the AFWA dog-friendly guides show sets that avoid pets.

While Oregon's rules are still enforced (details in the 2026–2028 furbearer booklet in Trapping\):

  • License: trapping needs an Oregon Furtaker's License — that includes "unprotected" animals like coyote, skunk, opossum and porcupine. A landowner can get a free license for land they own and live on (register the land with ODFW first).
  • Trapper education (free, online at hunter-ed.com/oregon/trapper) is required if you were born after June 30, 1968 or are trapping in Oregon for the first time — except on your own or family land, or when controlling damage to livestock or crops.
  • Every trap and snare must carry your ODFW brand number. Check traps at least every 48 hours.
  • Banned: toothed jaws; oversized footholds and land killing traps (size limits are in the booklet); game meat as bait; visible bait within 15 ft of a foothold set for carnivores. In the Rogue River-Siskiyou National Forest west of I-5 (that includes the Illinois Valley), no traps or snares hung in trees. On public land, stay 50 ft from trails and 300 ft from trailheads, campgrounds and picnic areas.
  • 2026–27 seasons: bobcat Dec 1–Feb 28 (needs a record card) · gray fox Oct 15–Feb 28 · raccoon, river otter and beaver Nov 15–Mar 15 · mink and muskrat Nov 15–Mar 31 · marten closed west of I-5 · fisher and ringtail closed all year.
  • Wolves are federally endangered here. Use coyote traps no bigger than #3 and snares with breakaways. A wolf track is about 5 in long; a coyote's is about 2.5 in (furbearer booklet, p. 16; side by side). Report any wolf caught by accident right away: ODFW, US Fish & Wildlife (503) 231-6179, or OSP (541) 664-4600.

🌱 Gardening and food

Climate at Cave Junction (NOAA 1991–2020 normals, station at 1,377 ft):

Typical date Play it safe
Last spring frost (32 °F) May 1 1-in-10 years it's after May 23
Last chilly night (36 °F) — matters in frost pockets May 28 1-in-10 after June 17
First fall frost (32 °F) Oct 12 1-in-10 years it's before Sep 23
Frost-free growing season ~161 days at least 133 days 9 years out of 10
  • USDA hardiness zone 8b (winter lows 15–20 °F) for Cave Junction, Takilma, Kerby, O'Brien and Selma — but valley-floor frost pockets run colder. Record low: −6 °F (1972).
  • About 61 in of precipitation a year, but only 5–7 in falls April–September, so summer gardens need irrigation (see the drip and water-wise guides).
  • Serpentine soils (common here — greenish rock, sparse "barrens"; photos): very high magnesium, low calcium, plus nickel and chromium, and they can contain natural asbestos.
    • Grow vegetables in raised beds with imported soil and lots of compost.
    • Wet the soil before digging, don't work it when it's dusty or windy, keep it mulched 3–6 in, and take shoes off indoors.
    • Get a soil test before adding amendments (don't add gypsum by default).
    • Some local clay soils drain slowly and stay waterlogged in winter, which is another reason for raised beds.
  • Local help: OSU Extension, Josephine County — (541) 241-3050; Master Gardener plant clinic Wednesdays 1–3 pm, (458) 291-7302.

⚡ Off-grid power and creek hydro

Start with the US Navy NEETS modules (electricity basics, AC, circuit protection, wiring, generators) in Off-Grid-Power\Electrical-Basics, then the solar guides, then the micro-hydro handbooks in Off-Grid-Power\Micro-Hydro.

Generators

  • Never run one indoors, in a garage, crawlspace or shed, even with doors open. Carbon monoxide kills in minutes. Keep it outdoors, away from windows and vents, and use battery-powered CO alarms.
  • Never plug a generator into a wall outlet ("backfeeding"). It sends power back through the lines and can kill anyone working on them. Wire into the house only through a transfer switch.
  • Store fuel outside living areas, in proper cans. Let the engine cool before refueling.
  • Engine won't start, carburetor gummed up, generator makes no power? See the Engines, Motors and Generators guide — it also covers chainsaws, rewinding motors, and turning motors and car alternators into generators.
  • Fuel: how long gas and diesel keep, rescuing stale or watery fuel, and getting fuel out of station tanks, drums and vehicles with no power: Fuel Guide.
  • EMP or solar storm: protecting solar gear, inverters and spares, and Faraday containers: EMP Guide.

Sizing a solar system (Sandia and IEA guides)

  • Panel size (kW) ≈ (kWh you use per day ÷ peak sun hours) × 1.2.
  • Lead-acid batteries: count on only 40% of the rated capacity. At our latitude, plan 7–9 days of storage for a solar-only system, and vent battery rooms at the top (hydrogen rises).
  • System voltage: 12 V under 1 kWh/day, 24 V for 1–4 kWh/day, 48 V above that. Low voltage over long wires needs very heavy wire.

Sizing creek hydro (NRCan, NCAT)

  • Real output (watts) ≈ flow (liters/second) × drop (meters) × 9.8 × 0.5. In US units: kW ≈ flow (cubic ft/min) × drop (ft) ÷ 709 × 0.5. Example: 50 gallons/minute falling 100 ft ≈ 470 W around the clock ≈ 11 kWh a day, more than solar gives most of the year here.
  • Design for late-summer low flow (the flow you have 95% of the time). Size the pipe so friction eats less than 10% of the drop. Screen the intake and leave water in the creek.
  • A hydraulic ram pump (Clemson plans) lifts creek water uphill with no fuel or power.

While Oregon's water laws are enforced: hydropower needs a state water right, and most Illinois Valley creeks are closed to hydro by state rule, including the Illinois River and its forks, Althouse, Sucker, Deer, Josephine, Rough and Ready and many more (OAR 690-515-0050). The OWRD guides and forms are in Micro-Hydro\.

Step by step — sizing solar for our cloudy winters, salvaged panels and batteries, wiring and fuses, lithium safety, and measuring a creek for hydro: Off-Grid Power.

🧼 Homestead skills: soap, fuel, heat, sanitation, crafts

Everything is in Homestead-Skills\ — start with the short Practical Action briefs (illustrated, step by step), then the longer manuals. For everyday necessities — cleaners, laundry soap, tooth powder, fire starters, oil lamps, glue, mending, tire sandals, pads and diapers — see the Make-Do Household Guide in this folder. For forging, heat-treating scrap steel, and making or sharpening hand tools, see Blacksmithing and Hand Tools.

Firewood (OSU, heat per cord when dry): Oregon white oak and madrone ≈ 30 million BTU, Douglas-fir ≈ 21, alder ≈ 18, ponderosa pine ≈ 17. Each 1% of extra moisture costs about 1% of the heat: split and stack wood through one dry summer (two is better) and keep it covered through the rains. A good stove is 60–80% efficient; an open fireplace 10–15%.

Carbon monoxide kills. Never run a generator, grill, charcoal, camp stove or a wood-gas unit indoors, in a garage or near windows. CO feels like flu without a fever. A battery CO alarm near the bedrooms is cheap insurance. A wood-gas generator keeps making CO-rich gas for ~20 minutes after the engine stops.

Lye (for soap and biodiesel): wear gloves and goggles, keep children away, add lye to cold water a little at a time (never water to lye), never use aluminum pots. On skin or in eyes: flush with lots of water for a long time (not vinegar).

Methanol (for biodiesel): very flammable, vapors creep along the ground to flames, absorbed through the skin, and swallowing it causes blindness. Work outdoors, away from any flame.

Stored fuel: gasoline stays good about 6 months (longer with stabilizer), diesel 6–12 months. Approved cans, outside the house, in a tub to catch spills.

Sanitation is what keeps disease away when water and sewer stop: the OSU twin-bucket toilet, then a proper latrine (Army FM 21-10, the WHO notes) at least 100 ft downhill from any well or creek, and handwashing with soap every time.

While laws are enforced: making fuel alcohol needs a free federal TTB permit first; Oregon requires permits for graywater systems and composting toilets/privies; and burning needs a Josephine County burn permit and a burn day ((541) 476-9663).

Births:

  • Goats and sheep: most deliver within 30–45 minutes of the water bag showing. Help if there's no progress after 30 minutes, if she strains with no water bag, or if a kid is mispositioned (the KSU and Langston guides show how to correct positions).
  • Newborn kids/lambs need colostrum, about 10% of body weight, within the first hour. Warm a chilled kid before feeding.
  • Cows: heifers should calve within 2–3 hours of the water sac, cows within 1–2. When pulling, loop the chain above and below the ankle and pull one leg at a time. The calf should nurse within an hour.

Horse colic (pawing, rolling, looking at the flank, no manure): don't force-feed, don't put a hose down the throat, don't repeat painkillers.

Humane euthanasia: see the illustrated UF guide (aim points by species) and the AVMA guidelines. Hold the firearm a few inches from the head; adult cattle and horses need a 9mm/.357 or larger — a .22 may not penetrate an adult skull.

Poisonous plants: most have no antidote. Poisoning mostly happens to hungry, stressed or newly moved animals — don't turn hungry stock onto weedy ground. Watch for tansy ragwort, poison and water hemlock, yew and oak (USDA color guide in Animals\Planning-...).

Predators: cougar, bear and coyote all take livestock here. Guardian dogs, solid night-time housing, electric fencing and not leaving feed out are the proven defenses (USDA guardian-dog guides, ODFW cougar and bear brochures).

Wildfire: evacuate animals early and in daylight; have 2–3 destinations. The Josephine County Fairgrounds (Grants Pass, (541) 476-3215) has sheltered evacuated livestock, and Southern Oregon Emergency Aid volunteers move livestock for free ((541) 226-1124 — call before it's urgent). If animals must stay: a grazed-down pasture, gates open, 7–10 days of water and feed.

Stock up now: since 2023, livestock antibiotics need a vet's prescription. Ask your vet now about a basic kit for your animals (wound care, dewormers, antibiotics, calcium for milk fever, electrolytes) — Drugs and Their Usage explains doses and withdrawal times.
