💧 Water Wells, Pumps, Springs & Cisterns

How to keep water coming when the power is out for good: understanding your well, running the well pump from a generator or solar, getting water up by hand, moving creek water uphill with no fuel (ram pumps), developing a spring, catching rain in a cistern, and keeping it all clean. The manuals are in Water-Wells-Pumps\ (Wells, Pumps-No-Grid, Hand-Ram-Rope-Pumps, Springs-Cisterns, Water-Quality, Oregon) and Water\. Making water safe to drink is in the Water Purification guide.

⚠️ Rules that prevent the worst problems

  • Never pour outside water into a well and never leave a well open — one bucket of creek water or a drowned animal can contaminate it (OWRD Wells and Drought).
  • Well pumps run on 240 volts. Shut off the breaker before touching the pressure switch or wiring, and connect a generator only through a transfer switch — never backfeed.
  • Don't run a flooded pump until it's dry and checked (EPA).
  • Test the water — clear, cold water can still carry bacteria or arsenic.

🕳️ Know your well

Three kinds of wells (USGS Ground Water and the Rural Homeowner, pp. 16–17):

  • Dug wells: wide and shallow, often lined with rock or concrete rings. Easy to contaminate and the first to go dry in drought — but you can lower a bucket into them.
  • Driven wells (sand points): a pipe with a screened tip driven into shallow sand or gravel. Cheap, shallow, easily contaminated.
  • Drilled wells: a steel or plastic casing (usually 6 in) sealed into the ground, often hundreds of feet deep, with an electric submersible pump at the bottom. Most valley homes have this kind. About 5 gallons a minute is a normal household supply.

Find your well's records. Oregon has driller's reports for most wells drilled since 1955 — Josephine County well IDs start with "JOSE". The report gives the depth, where water was found, the static water level and the tested yield — all things you need to plan a hand pump or generator (OWRD Water Well Owner's Handbook, pp. 27, 30). Print it and keep it with this drive. Look it up on OWRD's Well Log Query (by owner, location or well ID) while the internet works (rule of thumb).

What the ground here gives (Grants Pass area, the nearest USGS study): river gravels 5–50 gpm but easily polluted when shallow; weathered granite up to 50 gpm, solid granite under 5; serpentine rock — common around the Illinois Valley — gives few if any good wells, and high-magnesium water (USGS HA-480 Plate 1; Plate 2). In summer a household uses several times its winter water.

Measure your water level twice a year (spring and fall), from the same point on the casing, after the pump has rested several hours, and keep a log. A ¾-in slotted PVC measuring tube taped to the pump pipe lets a tape get past the wires (OWRD Handbook, pp. 15–16). A chalked weighted tape shows the wet line (rule of thumb).

Rules worth knowing: household use up to 15,000 gallons a day, livestock water and up to ½ acre of garden need no water right (OWRD Exempt Uses). Wells must be at least 50 ft from septic tanks and 100 ft from drain fields, with the top at least 12 in above ground (OWRD Handbook, pp. 23–24).

🔌 Running the well pump without the grid

From a generator (Franklin Electric AIM Manual, pp. 8–9) — a well pump needs far more power to start than to run:

Pump size Minimum generator — 2-wire pump 3-wire pump (has a control box)
½ hp 3 kW 2 kW
¾ hp 4.5 kW 3 kW
1 hp 6 kW 4 kW
1½ hp 7.5 kW 5 kW
  • Example: a ½ hp pump runs on about 670 watts but draws over 6 times that for a moment when it starts (AIM, p. 41). The pump's size is on the control box or the well report.
  • Start the generator first, then the pump; switch the pump off before stopping the generator. Never let the generator run out of fuel with the pump on (AIM, p. 8).
  • Pump into storage — run the generator an hour or two and fill tanks, instead of running it all day.

From solar: a DC solar well pump with its own panels pumps whenever the sun shines. Store water, not electricity — a tank holding about 3 days' water beats batteries for cost and upkeep (NRCS Solar-Powered Water Pump Systems, pp. 27, 29). Choose a brushless pump; brushed ones need new brushes about every 2 years (Practical Action, p. 4).

Pressure tank and switch (UNL G2152, pp. 2–3): the switch starts the pump at a low pressure (often 30 or 40 psi) and stops it at a high one (50 or 60). A bladder tank's air charge should be 2 psi below the start pressure, checked with the tank empty and power off.

Problem Usual cause (AIM, p. 28)
Pump starts and stops every few seconds Waterlogged tank (low air charge), leak, stuck check valve
Pump runs and won't stop Water level below the pump, worn pump, clogged screen, broken pipe
No water, pump silent Breaker, pressure switch contacts, control box, wiring
Sputtering, air in the water Water level dropping to the pump — rest the well, use less

✋ Getting water up by hand

The suction limit: a pump at the top of a well doesn't "lift" water — air pressure pushes it up. In practice a pitcher pump or any suction pump stops working at about 20–22 ft of lift, a little less at our elevation (USDA FB 1448, p. 27; Peace Corps Wells Construction, p. 262). Deeper than that, the pumping part must be down in the water — there's no limit on pushing water up from below.

Water level Options
Under ~20 ft Pitcher pump; rope pump; bucket and windlass on a dug well
20–150 ft Rope pump (to about 115 ft); deep-well lift handpumps (Afridev, India Mark II up to about 160 ft)
Deeper Generator or solar on the existing submersible; well bucket (below)
  • Rope pump: an endless rope with small pistons every meter runs up a pipe over a hand wheel. About 35 liters a minute at 33 ft, 10 at 115 ft; fits a 3-in borehole; can be built from local materials (Practica Rope Pump Handbook, pp. 8–9).
  • Deep-well handpumps: a cylinder set below the water on rods. Set the cylinder below the lowest water level you've measured while pumping (Afridev manual, p. 26; India Mark II manual). Commercial deep-well hand pumps can be fitted beside the electric pump in a 6-in casing (rule of thumb).
  • Well bucket (bailer): a narrow tube about 3 in wide with a flap valve in the bottom fits down a 4–6 in casing beside the pump pipe and brings up 1–2 gallons a trip on a rope and pulley. You must open the well cap and keep everything clean — sanitize the bucket and rope, and close the well between uses (rule of thumb; no manual found).

📚 Go deeper: Practical Action Human-Powered Handpumps, p. 5 — which pump for which depth; Practica Rope Pump Handbook — building one; Peace Corps Wells Construction — digging and lining a well by hand; Wikipedia: rope pump.

🔁 Ram pumps: creek water uphill with no fuel

A hydraulic ram uses the force of falling water in a long pipe to push part of that water much higher — day and night, with no fuel (Practical Action Hydraulic Ram Pumps).

  • Rule of thumb: water delivered ≈ water going in × fall ÷ lift × about 0.5. Example: 10 gallons a minute falling 20 ft into the ram can push about 0.8 gpm — roughly 1,200 gallons a day — up 100 ft (Peace Corps Hydrams, p. 66). Another: 3 gpm with a 12-ft fall lifts about 780 gallons a day to 48 ft (USDA FB 1448, pp. 24–25).
  • The drive pipe from the source to the ram must be straight, rigid (steel is best) and long — about 3–7 times the fall, and at least 100 times its own width (Practical Action, p. 3).
  • You need: a reliable flow and a few feet of fall — a creek with a small dam or a spring above the ram. The water that doesn't get pumped flows back out the waste valve.
  • Tune the waste valve's stroke for the best output; build one from pipe fittings with the Peace Corps manual.
  • Oregon water law and fish screens apply to diverting creek water — see the Engines guide creek-power section.

📚 Go deeper: Peace Corps Hydrams workshop manual — build and repair one; USDA FB 1448 — rams and windmills; Wikipedia: hydraulic ram.

🌬️ Windmills

Wind power rises with the cube of wind speed (twice the wind = 8 times the power); a water-pumping windmill pays off where the average wind is above about 7 mph, and needs a storage tank for calm days (Practical Action Wind Pumping, p. 2). Sheltered valley floors here may be too calm — ridges and open ground are better (rule of thumb).

📚 Go deeper: Practical Action Wind Pumping; USDA FB 1448 — windmills.

⛲ Springs

A spring box collects spring water before animals and surface runoff can foul it (Virginia Tech Spring Development, p. 5):

  • Dig back to where the water comes out of the ground and lay a gravel-packed collection pipe or trench.
  • A concrete box at least 4 ft deep, sticking up 1 ft above ground, with a tight overlapping lid, an outlet pipe, a screened overflow (so animals and insects can't crawl in) and a drain for cleaning.
  • Dig a ditch upslope to steer surface runoff away, and fence livestock out of the area above the spring; keep outhouses and corrals at least 100 ft away.
  • Measure flow with a bucket and a watch; test the water and shock-chlorinate a new box (Virginia Tech Shock Chlorination).
  • Spring water runs by gravity to the house or garden — no pump at all if the spring is uphill.

📚 Go deeper: Virginia Tech Spring Development and Protection; EPA Manual of Individual Water Supply Systems — springs, wells and cisterns.

🛢️ Cisterns and rainwater

Our winters are wet: every square foot of roof catches about 0.6 gallons per inch of rain (Texas Rainwater Harvesting, p. 34). A 1,500 sq ft roof in a 10-inch rain month could fill a 9,000-gallon tank (rule of thumb arithmetic).

  • First-flush diverter: dump the first dirty water off the roof — about 13–49 gallons per 1,000 sq ft (Texas, p. 13). A standpipe of 4-in pipe holds about 1 gallon per 18 in.
  • Size the tank for summer: almost no rain falls June–September, so store enough to carry you through.
  • Opaque, covered, screened tanks; clean gutters; treat roof water before drinking (Water Purification guide). Oregon rules for rain tanks: OSU EM 9448.

📚 Go deeper: Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting; OSU Harvesting Rainwater for the Garden.

🧪 Keeping well water clean

  • Test yearly for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and at least once for arsenic (treat at 10 ppb or more) (OWRD Handbook, p. 34). Without a lab, a home H₂S bacteria test helps — see the Water Purification guide.
  • Shock chlorination after repairs, floods or a bad test: for 5.25% bleach, about 2½ quarts for a 4-in well with 100 ft of water, 4 quarts for a 6-in well with 100 ft, 1½ gallons for 6-in with 200 ft. Run every tap until you smell chlorine, leave it 12–24 hours, flush it away from the septic system, and retest in 1–2 weeks (Virginia Tech Shock Chlorination, p. 3; OHA bulletin).
  • After a flood: wells under 50 ft deep or over 10 years old are probably contaminated (EPA, p. 1).
  • After a wildfire: check the wellhead, wiring and pipes, and test before drinking (OWRD after wildfire).
  • "Dry" well? Often the water only dropped below the pump — lower the pump (not to the very bottom) and fit a pump-saver switch (OWRD Wells and Drought).
  • Protect the wellhead: tight sanitary cap, ground sloping away, no fuel, manure or chemicals nearby (OSU Twelve Simple Things).

📚 Go deeper: OWRD Handbook — testing schedule, p. 34; Virginia Tech Shock Chlorination; OSU Twelve Simple Things to Protect Your Well Water.
