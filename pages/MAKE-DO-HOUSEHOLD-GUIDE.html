🧰 Make-Do Household Guide

How to make or replace everyday necessities when the store is gone — from basic supplies, things you can scavenge, and what grows around the Illinois Valley. Each section points to the detailed manuals in Homestead-Skills\ (especially Household-Make-Do\, Soap-Candles\ and Sanitation-Water\) and Electronics-Repair\.

⚠️ Rules that prevent the worst accidents

  • Never mix cleaning chemicals. Bleach + ammonia (or urine) makes toxic chloramine gas; bleach + vinegar or any acid makes chlorine gas. Use one product, rinse, then the next.
  • Lye (from ashes or the store) burns skin and blinds. Gloves and eye protection; flush any splash with plenty of water for 15 minutes. Never use aluminum pots with lye.
  • Label every container. Never store chemicals or fuel in food or drink bottles, and never reuse a container that held chemicals or fuel for food or water.
  • Never put gasoline in a lamp, stove or heater, and never burn charcoal, a camp stove or a generator indoors (carbon monoxide).

🧽 Cleaning and disinfecting

  • Most cleaning is just soap, hot water and scrubbing — it removes most germs along with the dirt. Homemade lye soap works fine (Soap-Candles\).
  • Scouring powder: baking soda, fine wood ash, or fine sand on a damp rag.
  • Glass and hard-water film: white vinegar mixed half and half with water (not on marble or stone, and never together with bleach).
  • Disinfecting with bleach (for kitchen surfaces, sickrooms, after flooding): plain unscented household bleach, 5 tablespoons (⅓ cup) per gallon of water, or 4 teaspoons per quart. Clean first, leave it wet for at least a minute, and mix it fresh every day — diluted bleach loses strength. Drinking water needs a much weaker dose — use the table in Water\EPA - Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water.
  • No bleach? Boiling water, scrubbing with soap, and drying things in full sun all kill germs.

👕 Laundry

  • Laundry soap: grate one bar of soap and mix with 1 cup washing soda (add 1 cup borax if you have it). Use 1–2 tablespoons per load, dissolved in hot water first.
  • Make washing soda from baking soda: spread baking soda in a pan and bake at 400 °F (200 °C) for about an hour, stirring now and then, until it turns dull and grainy. It's stronger than baking soda — wear gloves.
  • Ash water (old-time "lye wash"): soak hardwood ashes in water, let it settle, and use only the clear liquid for greasy or very dirty clothes. It's caustic — gloves, and never on wool or silk.
  • Washing by hand: soak overnight, agitate with a clean plunger in a bucket (or a washboard), rinse twice, wring hard, and dry in the sun — sunlight helps disinfect.

🪥 Personal care

  • Teeth: how you brush matters more than the paste. Brush twice a day with water, a little baking soda, or salt. No brush? Chew the end of a fresh green twig until it frays into bristles. Use thread as floss. (Medical\Manuals-PDF\Where There Is No Dentist)
  • Deodorant: wash daily; dust on baking soda or cornstarch (baking soda can irritate some skin).
  • Salve: melt 1 part beeswax with 4 parts oil or clean tallow (by weight) over low heat and pour into small tins. Lip balm is firmer: about 1 part wax to 2 parts oil.
  • Hair: wash with soap, then rinse with a cup of water with a spoonful of vinegar in it to remove soap film.

🩲 Menstrual pads and diapers

  • Reusable pads: several layers of absorbent cotton (old flannel or towel) sewn or folded into a pad, held in underwear. Rinse in cold water first, wash with soap, and dry in the sun. See Where Women Have No Doctor (Medical\).
  • Cloth diapers: absorbent cotton folded in layers, with a wool or plastic cover. Rinse, soak in a lidded bucket, wash hot with soap, dry in the sun.

🧻 Toilet paper alternatives

  • Water: a squeeze bottle of water, then wash your hands with soap — the cleanest option.
  • "Family cloth": cotton squares, used once, kept in a lidded bucket and washed hot, separately from other laundry.
  • Leaves: big soft thimbleberry leaves (common along roads and forest edges here in summer) or first-year mullein leaves.
  • ⚠️ Learn poison oak first — it's everywhere in the Illinois Valley. Leaves in threes, lobed like small oak leaves, shiny or dull, red in fall. The bare stems in winter still cause the rash. Also avoid stinging nettle.

🔥 Fire and light without stores

  • Fire starters: cotton balls rubbed with petroleum jelly; fatwood (resin-soaked heartwood from old pine or fir stumps — smells like turpentine and lights even when damp); dry bark and pine pitch.
  • Char cloth (catches a spark): put squares of 100% cotton cloth in a metal tin with a small nail hole in the lid, set it in a fire until smoke stops coming out, then let it cool before opening.
  • No matches: a ferro rod or flint-and-steel onto char cloth (steel struck on flint, quartz or pyrite throws sparks); a magnifying glass in sun; touching a 9-volt battery (or two AAs) to fine steel wool; or a bow drill — drill about ⅝ in thick and a foot long, of dry cedar, fir, cottonwood or basswood, on a board of the same wood with a notch cut to the center of the pit; tinder of dry grass with shredded cedar bark (Seton's Book of Woodcraft, pp. 192–194, and the Army survival manuals).
  • Oil lamp: a glass jar or tin, a wick of twisted cotton string or a cotton strip held up by a bent wire or a tin-lid holder, and any vegetable oil or melted fat. Keep the flame small. Never gasoline. Tallow candles: Soap-Candles\.

🪵 Glue, patching and waterproofing

  • Pine pitch glue: gently melt pine resin (it's flammable — heat beside the fire, not in it), stir in powdered charcoal (about 1 part to 3–4 parts resin) plus a pinch of fine plant fiber, and use while warm. Sets hard — for hafting tools, patching containers and seams.
  • Flour paste: cook flour and water to a thick paste — for paper and labels.
  • Waterproof cloth or leather: rub in beeswax, tallow or a beeswax–oil mix, then warm it so it soaks in.

👟 Clothing and footwear

  • Mending: running stitch for seams, backstitch where it must be strong, whipstitch for edges, and darning for holes (weave thread back and forth across the hole, then across the other way). Patch from the inside for strength. Needles from stiff wire; thread from unraveled fabric, dental floss or fishing line.
  • Sandals from old tires: trace your foot on tire tread, cut with a very sharp knife wetted with soapy water (steel belts make it slow — choose worn tread with no wire showing), sand the edges, and tie on with straps cut from an inner tube or with cord through slots.
  • Stay warm: wool keeps you warm even when wet; wet cotton pulls heat away — avoid it in cold rain.

🔧 Simple plumbing

  • Dripping faucet: shut the water off first, then replace the washer. A temporary washer can be cut from leather (cold taps) or rubber (hot taps).
  • Frozen pipe: open the faucet and thaw from the faucet end back toward the supply — never from the middle, where trapped water can burst the pipe. Thaw drains from the low end upward. (USDA Simple Plumbing Repairs.)

🔌 Electronics: repair and salvage

Manuals are in Electronics-Repair\ (and device-by-device guides in the iFixit archive in Kiwix). Start with the Navy safety manual and the Kuphaldt multimeter and soldering guides.

Safety first

  • Unplugged isn't safe. Capacitors hold a charge after unplugging. Discharge them with an insulated tool or shorting probe across the terminals and to the metal chassis, and recheck.
  • Microwave ovens can kill: the capacitor holds about 2,000 volts. Short it out before touching anything inside, never run the oven with the cover off, and don't break the magnetron's ceramic parts (toxic). Old tube TVs and monitors (CRTs) hold high voltage and can implode — leave them alone unless you know how.
  • Household 120 V can kill — especially with wet hands. Work with one hand when anything might be live.
  • Lithium batteries (laptops, phones, tools): throw out any that are swollen, dented, hot, or below about 2.5 volts. Charge only with a proper lithium charger, on a non-flammable surface, never unattended.

Finding the fault — check the simple things first: power cord and plug, fuse, switch, batteries and their contacts, loose wires. Then measure voltages, then test parts. Test a part in the circuit before you desolder it. Bulging or leaking capacitors are the most common failure in power supplies and chargers.

Multimeter quick tests

  • Continuity (beep): fuses, switches, cords, broken wires.
  • Diode: conducts one way only — about 0.5–0.7 V on the diode setting forward, nothing backward. Both ways = shorted; neither = open.
  • Transistor: test it like two diodes back to back (base-emitter, base-collector), and check collector-emitter reads high both ways.
  • Capacitor (discharged first): on the ohms setting a good one shows a brief "kick" then rising resistance; no kick = open, steady low reading = shorted.

Resistor color code: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, gray 8, white 9. Bands 1–2 are digits, band 3 is the number of zeros, the last band is tolerance (gold 5%, silver 10%). Example: yellow–violet–orange–gold = 47,000 Ω ±5%.

Soldering: use rosin-core solder (never acid-core plumbing solder); a 25-watt iron for circuit boards. Make the wires hold mechanically first, heat the joint, not the solder, and don't move it while it cools. Remove old solder with copper wick or a solder sucker.

What to salvage before throwing anything out:

  • Radios and clock radios: power transformers, cords, switches, knobs, speakers.
  • Computer power supplies: 12 V and 5 V power, fans, heat sinks, wire, connectors.
  • Printers and scanners: small motors, gears, rods, belts, switches.
  • Microwaves (after discharging!): fan and turntable motors, door switches, fuses, magnets.
  • Cars: batteries, alternators (12 V charging), lights, wire, fuses, relays — see Army TM 9-8000 in Manuals\ArmyFieldManuals.
  • Also: LEDs, magnet wire from motors and transformers, fuses, batteries, cords.
  • Motor run capacitors can be reused; motor start capacitors can burst if used continuously.

Crystal radio — no batteries needed (NBS Circular 120, 1922): a long wire antenna (about 75 ft, on insulators), a ground wire to a water pipe (never gas) or a rod in damp soil, a tuning coil wound on an oatmeal box, a germanium diode salvaged from an old radio as the detector, and a high-impedance earphone. It picks up AM stations with no power at all.

🍲 Hay-box cooking (saves fuel)

Bring the pot to a full boil, then nest it tightly in hay, wool, newspaper or blankets in a box with a padded lid; it keeps cooking for hours on its own heat. Bring food back to a boil before eating if it has cooled below hot (food is safest kept above 140 °F).

🫙 Containers and storage

  • Food-grade buckets (recycling number 1 or 2 on the bottom, from bakeries and restaurants), glass jars and metal cans with lids. Wash with soap, then rinse with the bleach solution above.
  • Keep mice out: metal cans or glass; hang sacks from wire. Set snap traps along walls.

💧 Clean water from plastic bottles (SODIS)

Fill clear plastic (PET) bottles of 2 liters or less with clear water, lay them on a roof or shiny surface in full sun for 6 hours (two full days if it's more than half cloudy). Doesn't work with cloudy water — let it settle or filter it first. Details: Sanitation-Water\ and Water\.

🐜 Pests

  • Roaches and ants: powdered borax mixed with sugar or flour, dusted into cracks (roaches), or a sponge soaked in borax-and-sugar syrup in their trail (ants). Keep borax away from children and pets.
  • Mosquitoes: screens, netting over beds, and smoke; homemade repellents are weak.
  • Lice: comb wet hair with a fine-tooth (nit) comb every day for two weeks; wash bedding hot.
  • Ticks: check every evening, especially after brush; pull straight out with tweezers.

For hundreds more recipes — cleaners, polishes, glues, inks, waterproofing, pest control and home remedies — see the classic formula books in Homestead-Skills\Household-Make-Do\. They are old: use their methods, but follow the modern safety rules above, and skip any recipe using mercury (bichloride), arsenic, cyanide, lead, benzene, carbon tetrachloride or carbolic acid, or any "remedy" taken by mouth that contains kerosene — those are poisons. The safe staples in the same books are soap, washing soda, borax, vinegar and absorbent powders.
