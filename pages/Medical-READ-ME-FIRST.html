🚑 The first few minutes

When someone is hurt, check in this order and act on the first problem you find (FM 4-25.11, p. 18–19; the newer Army order is MARCH — ATP 4-02.11, p. 53):

  1. Protect yourself — gloves or a clean plastic bag on your hands if there's blood.
  2. Responsive? Tap and ask "Are you OK?"
  3. Breathing? If not, open the airway and give rescue breaths (head-tilt, ATP p. 77; mouth-to-mouth, WTND p. 80).
  4. Bleeding? For heavy, life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, put a tourniquet on first — 2–3 inches above the wound, on bare skin, tight until the bleeding stops (it will hurt; that means it's tight enough), and write the time on the person (ATP 4-02.11 (2026), p. 62). For other bleeding, press hard on the wound with a thick, clean cloth (WHO-ICRC, p. 169), or pack the wound tightly where a tourniquet can't go (groin, armpit, neck — ATP, p. 69). The library's older FM 4-25.11 calls the tourniquet a "last resort" — that advice is out of date. Full steps: Injuries & Illness guide.
  5. Shock? Weak, fast pulse, pale cold sweat, confusion. Lay them down with feet a little raised (ATP, p. 99), keep warm; unconscious = on the side (ATP, p. 79; Recovery position) and nothing by mouth; awake and able to swallow = sips of rehydration drink, not plain water (WTND — shock; Injuries & Illness guide).
  6. Neck or back injury? Don't move them unless their life is in immediate danger (how to lift without bending, WTND p. 100).

There is no 24-hour ER in the Illinois Valley — see Local contacts in the Illinois Valley guide.

💊 Using found or expired medicines

1. Identify it. US pills must carry an imprint code. Open Pill-Identifier\PILL-IDENTIFIER.html, type the code, then check that the color, shape and size also match — the same code can be two different drugs (e.g. "I2" is ibuprofen 200 mg or amitriptyline 25 mg depending on size; "N 4" is nitroglycerin or a diet pill). No code at all = a supplement, a foreign or compounded product, or an illegal pill.

2. Never trust street pills. Pills that didn't come from a pharmacy can be counterfeits pressed with fentanyl — fake blue "M30" oxycodone, Xanax and Adderall are common, and many contain a deadly dose.

3. Look it up before giving it. In the WHO Model Formulary or MSF Essential Drugs (Medicines\), read what it treats, the dose, who must not take it, and interactions. For children, use the WHO Formulary for Children — children's doses go by weight. Give the right dose, no more and no less; finish every antibiotic course; antibiotics do nothing for colds (WTND, p. 56).

4. Expired medicines. Most tablets and capsules kept sealed, cool and dry lose potency slowly — military testing extended most stockpiled lots by years (88% of 3,005 lots, on average by 66 months), and some drugs kept 90% strength decades past their date (Zilker 2019, p. 54–55). That was warehouse storage, and inhalers and epinephrine often failed. But:

  • Don't rely on expired or badly stored: insulin (lasts only ~28 days unrefrigerated; never use if it froze), nitroglycerin tablets (lose strength once opened — keep in the original glass bottle; WHO: discard 8 weeks after opening, p. 276), epinephrine pens (photo; weaker when expired — but an expired pen with clear liquid is better than none in anaphylaxis), and mixed liquid antibiotics (amoxicillin suspension: 14 days in the fridge).
  • Throw out anything discolored, crumbling, sticky or smelling odd — especially tetracycline-family antibiotics, which can damage the kidneys when degraded. Also caked, brownish rehydration salts, and melted creams or suppositories (MSF, p. 648).
  • Don't raise the dose to make up for age — MSF warns of overdose. Heart and seizure medicines need exact doses, so respect their dates (p. 649).
  • Store medicines cool and dry (not the bathroom, kitchen or a car — a vehicle can reach 50–60 °C, MSF, p. 647), in their original containers, out of children's reach.

5. The biggest dangers

  • Allergy — face or throat swelling, trouble breathing or widespread hives after a medicine is anaphylaxis: give epinephrine if you have it. After any reaction, never use that medicine again (WTND, p. 70).
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) — no more than 4,000 mg a day for adults, counting every product; it's hidden in Norco, Vicodin, Percocet and many cold medicines. Too much causes liver failure (MSF, p. 244) — and an overdose needs urgent care even if the person feels fine (WHO, p. 68).
  • Opioids — a dose that's normal for someone used to them can stop the breathing of someone who isn't, or of a child. Never combine with benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin), alcohol or other sedatives. Naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid overdose — keep some.
  • Children — no cough/cold medicines under age 4 (US labels; never under 2), no aspirin for children or teens (Reye syndrome — WHO, p. 226).
  • Interactions — check the formulary when someone already takes blood thinners, heart medicines or antidepressants.
  • Poisoning: Oregon Poison Center 1-800-222-1222 (24/7, while phones work).

6. Measure it right (WTND ch. 8): 1 g = 1,000 mg · 1 mg = 1,000 mcg · 1 grain = 65 mg · 1 teaspoon = 5 ml · 1 tablespoon = 15 ml. Kitchen spoons vary — mark one at 5 ml measured with a syringe (WTND p. 61; cutting tablets: p. 60). Check the mg on the label: strengths differ. Never give medicine by mouth to someone asleep, unconscious or having a seizure (p. 62).

7. Getting rid of medicines — mix with coffee grounds or cat litter and seal; flush only what the FDA lists, like fentanyl patches — deadly to children even after use (FDA; FDA decision flowchart).

Stock up now: ask your doctor about extra supplies of the medicines your household depends on, and keep an emergency kit (wound care, pain/fever medicine, antihistamines, oral rehydration salts, naloxone).

💧 Diarrhea and dehydration: the homemade drink

Most children who die of diarrhea die of dehydration. Signs: thirst, little or dark urine, dry mouth, sunken tearless eyes, skin that stays up when pinched (the pinch test, WTND p. 151). Start the drink at the first watery stools (WTND, p. 151–152).

Rehydration Drink (drawn step by step, Hesperian Ch 5, p. 53; Oral rehydration): in 1 liter of clean water, stir ½ level teaspoon of salt and 8 level teaspoons of sugar. Taste before adding the sugar: it must be less salty than tears.

  • Give sips every 5 minutes until they urinate normally — 3 liters or more a day for a large person, at least 1 liter for a small child.
  • Keep giving it even if they vomit. Keep feeding, and keep breastfeeding babies.
  • No repeat doses of medicines that can build up until they urinate normally (WTND, p. 54).
  • Getting worse, or other danger signs? Get medical help.

🌿 Medicinal plants — go carefully

📷 Not sure what it is? Photograph it with the plant and mushroom identifier (🧮 Tools tab). It suggests names and flags poisonous groups, but it's often wrong: confirm every feature here before you eat or use anything.

Signs a home remedy probably doesn't work: many different "cures" for one illness, disgusting remedies, and anything using animal or human waste — that causes infections (WTND, p. 10–11).

Deadly look-alike: foxglove leaves have been mistaken for comfrey (foxglove photos, WSU p. 28), with fatal "comfrey tea" poisonings — and comfrey itself can harm the liver (FDA Bad Bug Book, p. 243). If in doubt, don't — and call the Poison Center.

📚 Go deeper: WHO Monographs Vol 1 — evidence plant by plant (Vols 1–4); WSU Poisonous Plants of the PNW, p. 28 — foxglove and other local poisons.

⚠️ Important

This is reference information, not a substitute for a doctor. Books and offline AIs can be wrong or out of date. Get professional help whenever it's possible; use this when it isn't, and double-check anything critical against more than one source.

Hesperian and MSF books are free for personal use; ask the publishers before giving copies to others.
