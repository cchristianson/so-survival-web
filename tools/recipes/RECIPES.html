Where these come from

Every recipe says where it came from: a section of one of this library's guides, or a book on the drive and the page it is on. Old books are fine for methods. For food safety — canning, curing meat, fermenting — follow the guides, because the times and pressures depend on how high up you are, and this page links to them rather than repeating them. Where an amount is ordinary kitchen knowledge with no source, the recipe says so. Nothing here is a quantity or a time that somebody made up.

A fair daily share

Per person per day, with one marked scoop per food. Two people present whenever the stores are opened, and a written log of what went out — the guide says why.

Use these first

Soonest to go off at the top. A date you entered beats the general guidance; where you gave no date, this counts from today using how long that food keeps sealed and cool.

Weigh-in

The ration on paper is not the ration people actually eat. Write everyone's weight down now and again every week or two: the first sign that someone is not getting their share is that they lose weight faster than the rest. Children and older people often give theirs away.

Where these numbers come from

What a person needs a day is the Food Security guide's table (the US Dietary Guidelines), planned from the top of each range because "active" there only means walking three miles a day. The cold-weather addition and the 2,100 kcal planning figure are the WHO's, through the same guide. How long food keeps sealed comes from Utah State Extension through that guide's storage table. Calories, protein and fat per food are USDA FoodData Central's standard figures for the ordinary form of each food — a planning figure, not a measurement of your sack of wheat. Three wild foods (camas, cattail, wild seed) are not in USDA's tables at all and are marked as estimates wherever they appear.

This page does not decide anything for you. It will not tell you to cut a child's food, it does not know who is ill or pregnant beyond what you typed, and it cannot see the mould in the bottom of the bucket. Hunger diseases, feeding someone who is already starving, and babies in a shortage are all in the Food Security guide, and this page sends you there rather than repeating them.
